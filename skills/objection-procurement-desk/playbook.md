# The Objection and Procurement Playbook

This is the method behind the desk. The Scope and Pricing playbook explains how proposals are built; this one explains what to do when the answer comes back as anything other than yes.

The core belief: pushback is rarely about the thing it says it is about. "Too expensive" is the most socially acceptable sentence in business, and it is used to carry budget problems, risk anxiety, internal politics, process requirements, and simple habit. The studio that answers the sentence instead of the situation concedes money to fix problems that money does not fix. Diagnose first, respond second, concede last and only in trade.

A note on posture throughout: the buyer across the table is usually not the enemy and often not even the decision maker. They are a person who has to defend this purchase inside a building you cannot see. Almost everything in this playbook is designed to arm that person while protecting the studio.

## Part 1. The objection library

Each entry follows the same shape: what it usually means, the questions that reveal the real blocker, the response structure, and the walk-away line. Rates, floors, and term limits referenced throughout live in the studio's rate and rules sheet and nowhere else.

### 1. "Too expensive" / "Finance needs X percent off"

**What it usually means.** Four possibilities, in rough order of frequency. A genuine budget ceiling the speaker did not set. A procurement or finance policy that requires a negotiated reduction to be recorded, any reduction. A risk objection in disguise: they are not sure enough to defend the full number. Or a habit: this buyer asks everyone, every time, because asking has never cost them anything.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "If the number worked, is everything else in the proposal agreed?" (Isolates price from every other doubt. If they hesitate, price was never the real issue.) "Is X percent a policy figure, or is there a total budget the scope needs to fit?" (Separates policy theatre from a hard ceiling, and invites the budget, which is far more useful than the percentage.) "Who else has to approve at the full number that would not at the reduced one?" (Maps the approval chain the discount is really buying passage through.)

**Response structure.** Acknowledge without apology. Isolate with the first question. Then answer with structure, not movement: the scope trade menu from the scope skill (fewer SKUs in phase 1, fewer rounds, no production liaison, longer timeline removing any rush line), each trade with its price effect. Offer to design to a stated budget as a tier, full menu visible. If a non-money concession is needed, trade it: faster payment, case study permission, a phase 2 commitment, an introduction.

**Walk-away line.** "If the full scope at a reduced rate is the only version that works on your side, we are not the right studio for this project, and I would rather tell you now than deliver it badly at a number that does not fund the work." Deliver warmly, once, and mean it.

### 2. "We can do this in-house now, with AI / Canva / our junior designer"

**What it usually means.** Sometimes a genuine capability belief, increasingly sincere since AI tools improved. More often: a budget argument wearing a capability costume, or a buyer testing whether the studio believes its own price. Occasionally it is true for part of the scope (routine adaptations) and false for the parts that carry the risk (architecture, structural, regulated content, print).

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "Which parts were you picturing in-house?" (Forces the scope conversation; the answer almost always concedes the hard parts back.) "Who owns it when the retailer rejects the artwork or the dieline is wrong?" (The accountability question. In-house tools produce files; they do not absorb consequences.) "What is driving it, budget or control?" (Asked kindly, this gets an honest answer surprisingly often, and each answer has a different response.)

**Response structure.** Agree where agreement is true: yes, adaptation and templated work is increasingly automatable, and the proposal already prices that reality (per-SKU adaptation rates exist precisely because masters carry the value). Reframe around what is actually being bought: the architecture decisions, production fluency (substrates, print processes, colour limits), regulatory placement, and someone accountable at the reset deadline. Offer the hybrid structure if it is real: studio does strategy, masters, and guidelines; client team does adaptations inside the system, with a support block. That offer is a scope design, not a retreat, and it frequently becomes the retainer shape later.

**Walk-away line.** "If the in-house route covers it, take it with our blessing. If the launch gets into trouble at artwork stage, call us then; we would rather rescue a range than watch one miss its reset." Gracious, and it leaves the studio as the obvious second call.

### 3. "Your incumbent / competitor quoted less"

**What it usually means.** A comparison of unlike scopes, a genuine incumbent advantage (they already hold the files and the relationships), or a number quoted to you that may not survive being asked about twice. Incumbents also price defensively when they smell a switch, and their cheapest quote is often the one given to keep you out, not the one the work will finish at.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "What does their number include?" (Ask without edge. Rounds, production support, source files, and guidelines are the usual silent exclusions.) "Is the decision price, or is price the tiebreak?" (If the taste buyer prefers your work, this surfaces it.) "If we match the scope line by line at our rates, is the comparison still against us?" (Moves the fight from their number to the deliverables list, which is ground the studio can win on.)

**Response structure.** Never litigate the competitor's business model and never match blind. Walk your own deliverables list, name what the comparison likely excludes, and offer the matching reduced scope at the matching price: if their quote is for masters only, price masters only. Where the incumbent's advantage is real (file custody, history), answer it with the switching costs handled explicitly: transition support, file audit, a first-project structure that de-risks the move.

**Walk-away line.** "If it is purely the lowest number, we will lose that contest on purpose; our price is what the work costs done properly. If the deliverables matter, let us compare lists and I will show you exactly where the two quotes differ."

### 4. Day-rate benchmarking by procurement

**What it usually means.** Process, almost always. Procurement is measured on recorded comparisons and rate variances, and a day rate is the only unit their spreadsheet accepts for creative work. The benchmark is usually a freelance marketplace or an artwork-only supplier, categories that exclude strategy, structural design, production accountability, and revision structure by construction.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "Which roles are you benchmarking, against which supplier type?" (The answer usually reveals the benchmark is an artworker.) "Is the rate card a gate we must pass, or a record you must file?" (Very different problems: a gate needs escalation and possibly a founder decision; a record needs good paperwork.) "Would a deliverables-based price with the rate card filed alongside satisfy the process?" (Often yes, and this is the exit.)

**Response structure.** Do not defend the rate line by line; that accepts the spreadsheet's framing. Redirect to deliverables and outcomes: the proposal is a price for named outputs with named protections, and the day rate is an internal costing unit, not the product. Supply the rate card cover note (template) that gives procurement their record: roles, rates, what each rate carries (insurance, revision structure, production experience, accountability), and the deliverables basis of project pricing. If a rate must be contextualised, contextualise by role mix: the blended team on this project is not the single benchmarked role.

**Walk-away line.** "If the panel decision is made on a single day-rate line against freelance artwork rates, we cannot win it honestly, and we will not win it dishonestly by reclassifying the work. Here is our rate card and our deliverables pricing; if the process allows both to be considered, we are in."

### 5. "We need it faster" (speed as the objection)

**What it usually means.** Either a real external deadline (retailer reset, launch, board date), in which case speed is a scope and price question, or internal drift being converted into the studio's emergency. Diagnose which before quoting anything: a deadline that has existed for three months and arrives as a rush in week twelve is a planning failure the client is asking the studio to fund.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "What date is fixed, and what fixes it?" "What happens commercially if it slips a week?" (If the answer is vague, the rush is soft.) "Which parts must land by the date, and which can follow?" (Most rushes are one deliverable wearing a whole project's coat.)

**Response structure.** Use the scope skill's rush mechanics: multiplier from the rate sheet applied to affected phases, shown as a visible line, with the standard timeline at the standard price offered beside it. Offer the phased landing: the critical SKUs or the hero pack by the date, the balance on a normal timeline at normal rates. Never absorb a rush silently; an unpriced rush teaches the client's planning system that the studio is the contingency plan.

**Walk-away line.** "We can hit the date at the rush structure, or the scope at the standard date. What we cannot do is the full scope, at the standard price, in half the time; that version ends with errors at print, and neither of us wants our names on it."

### 6. "I need to get this past the board / Finance / my boss"

**What it usually means.** Frequently the truth, and the most useful objection on this list: your buyer is selling internally and losing. The blocker is not in your inbox; it is in a meeting you will never attend. Treat the buyer as your channel, not your opponent.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "What will they push on?" "What would make this an easy yes in that room: a smaller first phase, a pilot, different payment timing?" "Would it help if I gave you a one page version built for that audience?" (It almost always would.)

**Response structure.** Build the internal selling kit: a one page summary written for finance eyes (total, phases, what each phase buys, payment timing, the cost of delay where a reset or launch is real), a phased entry point (paid discovery or phase 1 with a decision gate), and payment structuring inside the rate sheet's terms if timing is the true blocker. Give the buyer language for the objections they will face, drawn from this same library. Check in on the meeting date, and log the internal deadline as the real next step.

**Walk-away line.** Rarely needed here. The failure mode is not walking away; it is discounting to help the buyer internally. Do not. A smaller phase helps them and protects the rate; a discount helps them once and re-prices the studio forever.

### 7. "Just send us some concepts and we will decide"

**What it usually means.** Either a buyer who genuinely believes creative work is auditioned like a product demo, or a harvest: free strategy and routes gathered from several studios, with the work (or the thinking) landing with an incumbent or in-house. The more polished and process-heavy the request, the more seriously to take the second reading.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "What will the concepts be judged on, and by whom?" "Is there a budget approved for the full project, and a decision date?" "What happens to the routes you do not choose?" Vague answers to all three is the diagnosis complete.

**Response structure.** Run the scope skill's spec work tree. Decline free creative warmly and in one sentence, then offer the paid discovery structure: fixed price, dated, deliverable is the audit, architecture, and the scoped plan, creditable against the project if that is the studio's policy. If a formal pitch inside the studio's limits is genuinely warranted, scope the pitch itself: one route, concept level, presented not sent, IP protected (drafting aid, counsel review), decision date diarised.

**Walk-away line.** "We do not send concepts ahead of an engagement. It is how we keep our paying clients' work at the front of the queue, and it is the same protection your work would get from us."

### 8. Net-90 and terms objections ("Our standard terms are...")

**What it usually means.** Process again: a large organisation's accounts payable machine runs on its own calendar, and the person telling you is rarely the person who set it. But "standard terms" is also used as a genuine negotiation lever, and every term is money: payment days are financing the studio provides, kill fees are insurance, and liability caps are risk transfer. Terms objections are price objections in a suit.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "Is that a policy with no exceptions, or the default an exception can be requested against?" "Who can approve an exception, and what do they usually need to see?" "What is your actual payment run in practice?" (The stated terms and the lived terms often differ, in both directions, and the scope skill's payment section explains why this answer is a scoping input.)

**Response structure.** Answer terms with structure, not surrender: milestones sized so the studio's exposure at any moment is small, deposit and purchase order before start, balance before final file release, late terms and pause rights from the rate sheet. Where the client's terms are genuinely immovable and the client is worth it, that is a founder decision taken with the cash cost stated in numbers, not a concession made in an email. Use the terms response template so the reply addresses every clause they raised, accepts what is acceptable explicitly, and counters what is not, rather than accepting a whole document by silence.

**Walk-away line.** "We can work inside a lot of structures, but we cannot finance a quarter of a year of a large company's production from a small studio's cash flow. If the terms cannot move at all, the honest answer is that the fit is wrong, on process rather than on the work."

### 9. IP buyout and source file demands

**What it usually means.** Three different wants sharing one sentence. A legitimate operational need (their team must adapt files across markets), an asset grab by a thorough procurement function doing its job, or a buyer who does not know what native files are and is reading from a checklist. Diagnose before pricing the answer.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "What do you need to be able to do with the files?" (The answer defines the product: adaptation rights, templates, and full assignment are three different prices.) "Which formats, and who will work in them?" "Is this for this range only, or the brand system as a whole?"

**Response structure.** Follow the scope skill's source file tree and IP sections: final production files are the standard deliverable; native files, templates, and broader rights are priced lines with licence caveats (fonts, stock, plugins travel under their own licences). Sell the specific extension they need, not the universe. Every IP commitment is flagged as drafting aid for counsel review, and nothing about IP is agreed verbally.

**Walk-away line.** "Full assignment of everything, including the routes you did not choose, is not something we sell at any price; it is the studio's working capital. What we can sell is everything you need to run this range without us, and here is that structure."

### 10. Exclusivity and category conflict demands

**What it usually means.** A client buying insurance against their competitor getting the same studio. Sometimes reasonable and payable; sometimes an enormous ask made casually ("you would not work with anyone else in snacks, obviously") that would cost the studio a category for one project's fee.

**Questions that reveal the blocker.** "Which competitors, named, and which adjacent categories?" (Exclusivity is priced by its perimeter; vague perimeters are refused or narrowed, never accepted broadly.) "For how long, and what volume of work sits behind it?" "Is this a condition of this project, or the start of a retainer conversation?" (The honest version of exclusivity is usually a retainer with a defined category clause.)

**Response structure.** Never agree exclusivity inside a project negotiation email. Escalate to the founder with the perimeter, duration, and the opportunity cost estimated from the studio's pipeline. If offered at all, it is: named competitors only, a defined category narrow enough to survive contact with the studio's other work, a fixed term, a consideration (retainer minimum or an exclusivity fee), and counsel reviewed language. An unpaid, open-ended, broadly defined exclusivity is a walk-away trigger regardless of the project fee.

**Walk-away line.** "We cannot sign away a whole category for a single project; that is our pipeline you are asking for. If exclusivity matters, let us structure it properly: named competitors, a set term, and a retainer that makes it rational for both sides."

## Part 2. Procurement processes, end to end

Brand directors fall in love; procurement functions process. A small studio survives procurement by understanding that each process below has two tracks running at once: the stated track (forms, panels, criteria) and the sponsorship track (whether anyone inside wants you specifically). The stated track decides whether you may be bought; the sponsorship track decides whether you are. Work both, never confuse them, and never enter the stated track without a sponsor if you can help it: the packaging vertical layer's rule holds, never let procurement be the first conversation.

### Panels and approved supplier lists

**How they run.** The organisation maintains a roster per category. Entry is periodic (often annual), competitive, and paperwork led: company details, insurance certificates, financial standing, references, rate cards. Once on the panel, individual projects may still be mini-competed among panellists.

**How a small studio survives.** Price the admin before applying: hours of paperwork, insurance upgrades if any, and the probability-weighted value of the work the panel actually controls. Apply when a brand-side sponsor confirms real volume flows through the panel and names upcoming work; decline politely when the panel is a fishing expedition with no named projects. Keep panel paperwork in a reusable folder (certificates, policies, standard answers) so the second application costs an hour, not a week. Once on a panel, behave like it is an audition that never closes: small jobs done impeccably are what convert roster membership into flagship work.

### RFPs

**How they run.** A written brief, a question deadline, a submission format, an evaluation panel, sometimes a paid or unpaid pitch stage, and a decision date that frequently slips. Scoring is usually criteria based on paper, and relationship influenced in fact; both are true at once.

**How a small studio survives.** Triage every RFP before writing a word (templates/rfp-triage-scorecard.md). The fatal RFPs share tells: no named budget, no access to the decision maker, creative required at submission, a timeline that suggests the decision is already made, an incumbent "also invited". Bid only where the score clears the studio's threshold and a human inside will answer questions. Answer the questions asked, in the format asked, with the deliverables language from your scope structures; evaluation panels score compliance first and brilliance second. Diary the decision date and follow up once, properly, then close the file on your side; RFP losses consume studios through hope, not effort.

### Vendor onboarding

**How they run.** After selection, before any purchase order: supplier registration portals, tax forms, bank details, insurance evidence, sometimes security or sustainability questionnaires, occasionally a demand to accept the client's standard terms wholesale as a condition of the portal account.

**How a small studio survives.** Treat onboarding as part of the deal, not an afterthought: the terms response (template) is filed during onboarding, not at invoice time. Read portal terms for the clauses that matter (payment days, IP defaults, liability, termination) and counter them through the terms process the client itself provides; portals feel absolute and usually contain an exceptions route. Never start work on a verbal "you are basically onboarded". The purchase order is the start line; onboarding complete plus PO in hand is the rule the scope playbook sets, and it is repeated here because procurement timelines are where it gets tested.

### Rate cards

**How they run.** Procurement asks for a standard rate card: roles, day or hourly rates, sometimes volume discount grids. The card is filed, benchmarked, and produced later in negotiations as if it were a promise that every project is bought by the day.

**How a small studio survives.** Submit the card; it is the price of playing, and refusing reads as either arrogance or disorganisation. Submit it with the cover note (template) that frames it: rates are costing units for time-based work; project work is priced on deliverables; the card's own terms (validity period, review date, what rates include) are stated on the card itself. Date and version every card. A rate card with an expiry is a negotiation asset; one without is a permanent anchor thrown by past you at future you.

### Framework agreements

**How they run.** A master agreement covering all future work: terms, IP, liability, rates or rate mechanism, term and termination. Individual projects then run under short statements of work referencing it. Negotiation is front loaded and legal heavy for a small studio; the payoff is that every later project starts faster and argues less.

**How a small studio survives.** This is counsel territory; the desk prepares the commercial summary and the list of clauses that carry money, and the founder signs nothing framework shaped without legal review, flagged as such. The clauses that matter commercially: payment days and late terms; IP defaults (watch for "all work product including concepts" language, which converts every pitch and unused route into their property); liability caps and indemnities disproportionate to project fees; exclusivity or most-favoured-pricing clauses (a promise that they get your best rate forever is a slow leak, and comparing across clients brushes against the confidentiality rule in reverse); termination for convenience with no kill protection. A framework that prices those fairly is a genuine asset. One that does not is a panel you joined to lose money at scale.

## Part 3. Negotiation mechanics

**Trade, never give.** A concession offered alone is read as evidence the price was soft. A concession exchanged is read as a deal being built. Every movement from the studio's side names its price: scope for money, speed for rush terms, terms for volume or commitment, signature for deposit. Use the sentence shape: "We can do X, if on your side Y works." If the other side's Y is nothing, the answer is not yet a trade; wait.

**Anchoring.** The first complete structure on the table anchors the negotiation, which is why the proposal goes out as tiers with a recommended option rather than a single number awaiting attack. In counteroffers, re-anchor on structure when the conversation collapses to a single figure: restate the deliverables list, the phases, and the protections, and let the number be the sum of visible parts. A number with a visible anatomy is far harder to halve rhetorically than a bare number.

**Silence.** After making an offer or asking the diagnostic question, stop. Silence is where the buyer reveals the real blocker: they fill it with the reason, the name of the true decision maker, or the actual constraint. The studio side loses more money talking through its own offers (adding unrequested justifications, then unrequested sweeteners) than in any other single behaviour. In drafts, this means: send the complete reply and do not send the follow-up paragraph ten minutes later. In call scripts, it is written in as an instruction: ask, then wait.

**Concede in decreasing steps, slowly.** If movement is authorised at all, each step is smaller than the last and slower than the last, which signals approach to a real limit. Steps of equal size signal a staircase with more steps. And every step is a trade per the rule above; the size rule governs the shape, the trade rule governs the content.

**Document as you go.** Confirm each agreement in writing the day it is made, briefly: "Confirming: phase 1 at [fee], two rounds, deposit [terms]; source files remain excluded, priced separately if wanted." This is not mistrust; it is how both sides survive staff changes, memory drift, and the six weeks between agreement and invoice. The negotiation log (template) is the internal version; the confirmation email is the external one. Deals die at documentation stage more often than at price stage, usually because what each side documented privately was a different deal.

**Deadlines.** Use real ones, name fake ones kindly. A retailer reset is a real deadline and a legitimate reason for structure to firm up. "We sign Friday" from a buyer who has said it three times is weather, not climate; respond completely and same day, but do not let the calendar edit the terms.

## Part 4. Internal escalation

The desk's authority is written in the rate and rules sheet: which trades the account executive may offer, which numbers they may not touch, and what must go to the founder. Escalation is mandatory, before any response is sent, when:

- The ask touches the rate itself, or would take the deal below the founder's floor.
- Terms move outside the sheet: payment days beyond the stated limit, kill fee removal, uncapped liability, IP assignment beyond standard, exclusivity in any form.
- The client asks for another client's terms, rates, or deal structure (also a hard refusal; see standing rule 3, and the refusal itself needs no escalation, only the logging does).
- A walk-away is being considered in either direction: the client threatens it, or the studio should.
- Anyone on the studio side has already made a conflicting commitment, discovered mid-negotiation.

An escalation is a one page brief, not a forwarded thread: the situation in three lines, the diagnosis (price, process, risk, or politics) and its evidence, what has been offered and conceded so far (from the negotiation log, which is why the log exists), the specific decision needed, and the desk's recommendation with the reasoning. Founders decide fastest and best from briefs; they decide worst from panic and forwarded email chains at midnight.

After the decision, the response goes out from the desk as one voice. The client never learns there was an internal argument, a floor, or an escalation; "I have checked and we can..." is as much of the machinery as they ever see.

## Closing note

The desk wins by being the calmest, clearest, best documented party in every exchange. Studios do not lose negotiations because their work was not good enough. They lose them in the ten days after the proposal, one small undocumented concession at a time. Classify, diagnose, trade, document. That is the whole craft, practised consistently.
