# Example 2: Procurement Day-Rate Benchmarking

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative. Rates are from the fictional rate sheet shown in this example.

## The studio (fictional rate sheet excerpt)
Hartwell Studio. Roles: creative director 950/day, senior designer 750/day, designer 550/day, artworker 450/day, project manager 500/day.
- Project work is priced on deliverables. Day rates apply to time-based work and change orders
- Discount authority: scope trades only. Rate card version 4, valid to year end

## Situation
Hartwell has been selected by the brand team at Calderbrook Foods (fictional, mid-size food group) for an 18 SKU range redesign. Before onboarding completes, procurement sends the email below. The brand director, the sponsor, has gone quiet since it arrived.

## Messy input (procurement email)
"As part of vendor setup we benchmark all creative suppliers. Your senior designer rate of 750/day is 36 percent above our benchmark for design resource (550/day, freelance market data). Please align your senior and designer rates to benchmark so we can complete onboarding. The project fee will then be recalculated on aligned rates."

## Reasoning
Classify: rate benchmarking, playbook entry 4. Note the closing move: "the project fee will then be recalculated" converts a deliverables price into a day rate sum, which is the real objective.
Diagnose: Process. This is a category manager executing a recorded comparison; the 550 figure is an artworker-adjacent freelance rate being applied to a senior designer title. There is no evidence the brand side wants a cheaper studio; they already chose.
Diagnosis risk: if the studio defends rate by rate, it accepts the recalculation premise and loses the deliverables basis of the whole proposal. If it refuses rudely, it strands its sponsor, who now has an onboarding problem.
Response shape: rate card cover note attached, polite refusal of "alignment" as a concept, the role mix point, the deliverables basis restated, and a practical offer: a walk through of the proposal's staffing so procurement can file an informed benchmark note. Also, quietly, a separate short message to the sponsor alerting them, because their project timeline is what an onboarding stall eats.

## Output: the reply (client facing)
"Thank you for setting out the benchmark clearly. We cannot align the rates, and I want to explain why in terms your file can use, because the comparison is measuring two different products.
Our 750 senior designer rate is a fully loaded studio rate: employment costs, insurance, software and font infrastructure, and the revision structure and production accountability our proposals carry. The 550 benchmark is a freelance day rate, which excludes nearly all of that, and (we would gently note) sits at our designer level, not senior. On this project the staffing blend is what matters: across the 18 SKUs, roughly a third of the time is senior design, a third design and adaptation, a third artwork, production, and management, and the project price was built on deliverables: 18 SKUs, two families, two rounds per phase, production liaison included.
I have attached our rate card (version 4) with its cover note, which sets this out for your records. If it would help the file, I can walk your team through the proposal's staffing and deliverables for twenty minutes, so the benchmark note reflects the blend rather than a single title. What we cannot do is reprice a deliverables proposal as a sum of day rates; the protections in the price (rounds, liaison, file standards) do not survive that conversion, and the brand team would feel the difference at artwork stage.
Happy to proceed on that basis, and to complete the rest of onboarding this week."

## Outcome (illustrative)
Procurement filed the cover note, noted the benchmark variance as "explained: deliverables based supplier", and onboarding completed without rate movement. The sponsor resurfaced once the process closed, which is the normal choreography: taste buys, process files, work starts.

## Critique
Strengths: the reply gave procurement a fileable outcome (a recorded, explained variance is a completed task for a category manager), refused the recalculation premise rather than the people, and never disclosed anything about any other client. The separate sponsor note was not in this email and should not be; mixing the two audiences in one message would have embarrassed both.
Watch outs: the blend description ("roughly a third...") must match the studio's actual staffing on the proposal or it becomes the kind of invented precision edge case 14 in the scope skill warns about. Pull the real blend from the estimate before sending anything like this.
