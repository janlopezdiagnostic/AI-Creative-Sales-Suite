# Example 3: "We Can Do This In-House With AI Now"

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative.

## The studio (fictional rate sheet excerpt)
Hartwell Studio. Master design per family and per-SKU adaptation rates as per the proposal below. Source files excluded, priced per family. Guidelines and template systems priced as their own phase. Discount authority: scope trades only.

## The proposal they are answering
Fernlight Skincare (fictional DTC brand): identity refresh applied to packaging for 4 products, total 9,400: strategy application 1,200; identity to packaging system 3,100; master artwork 4 SKUs 2,900; guidelines and templates 2,200.

## Messy input (founder, on a call, as noted by the AE)
"Look, I will be honest. Since we last spoke our ops manager has been playing with Canva and some AI tools and honestly the mockups look... fine? Like, 80 percent there. My co-founder is asking why we would pay nine grand when she can get us most of the way. What am I actually buying from you that she cannot produce?"

## Reasoning
Classify: in-house alternative, playbook entry 2, with an honest capability question inside it. The founder is not bluffing and not hostile; he is asking the question his co-founder will ask him, which makes this an internal selling moment as much as an objection.
Diagnose: Risk, primarily: he cannot yet articulate the difference between mockups that look fine and packaging that survives production, retail, and regulation. Secondary: genuine budget curiosity.
What not to do: disparage the tools (he has seen the mockups; telling him they are bad tells him his eyes are bad), recite the portfolio, or drop the price.
Response shape: concede the true 80 percent honestly (layout exploration, simple adaptations, mockups for internal discussion are genuinely cheap now), then name precisely what the remaining 20 percent consists of in his world: packaging architecture across 4 products that must read as a range, regulated content placement on a skincare pack (claims, ingredients, warnings: the client owns accuracy, the studio owns correct placement), dieline and print reality (his printer's constraints, colour on the actual substrate), and accountability at the point the printer or a retailer says no. Then offer the hybrid: studio builds the system, masters, and the template guidelines; his ops manager produces routine adaptations inside it, with a support block. That converts the objection into a scope design. It also makes the guidelines phase more valuable, not less: in the hybrid, the guidelines are what his team works from every day.

## Output: call script (excerpt, as prepared for the AE)
Opening: "That is the right question, and I am not going to pretend the tools have not changed things. Let me split the job the way I would if I were spending your money."
Concede: "The mockup stage, trying directions, simple resizes, your ops manager can do well, and honestly some of the adaptation work on a stable range is heading the same way. If the proposal priced that as senior craft, I would be worried too."
Name the 20 percent: "What the fee actually buys is the part the tools do not carry: the system underneath, how four products read as one range on a shelf and a thumbnail; the regulated content placed correctly, because skincare claims and warnings are not decoration and the liability sits with the brand; the print reality, your printer's dieline, colour behaviour on your substrate, files that pass first time; and a named studio accountable when the printer or a retailer pushes back, which happens on most launches."
Offer the hybrid: "So here is a structure that matches how you actually work. We build the system and the four masters, and the guidelines become real working templates, designed for your ops manager to adapt from. Adaptations after launch sit with your team, with a small support block from us for the tricky ones. That takes the proposal from 9,400 to [rebuilt figure from the scope skill], and honestly it is how I would set this up if you were my brand."
Wait. (Written in the script: after the offer, stop talking. His next sentence tells you whether the blocker was risk or budget.)

## Outcome (illustrative)
The founder took the hybrid. The guidelines phase grew slightly (real templates, tested with the ops manager in a handover session), launch adaptations moved in-house as proposed, and the studio kept strategy, masters, and the support block. Six months later the brand's first retailer pitch came back with packaging questions the in-house route could not answer, and the support block became a small retainer.

## Critique
Strengths: the answer respected his intelligence and his co-founder's question, which is why he could repeat it at home. The concession was real, which made the boundary credible: a studio that admits what is cheap is believed about what is not.
Watch outs: the hybrid only works if the templates are genuinely usable by a non designer; a guidelines document written for designers would have stranded his ops manager and proved his co-founder right. Hybrid offers must be scoped by the scope skill with the handover session included, not promised on the call as a vibe.
