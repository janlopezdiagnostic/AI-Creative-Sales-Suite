# Edge Cases

Format: trigger, the right move, the wrong move. This file covers the negotiation and procurement edge; scoping edge cases (SKU growth, revision creep, late regulatory copy) live in the Scope and Pricing Architect and are not repeated here. Rates, floors, and term limits always come from the studio rate sheet.

**1. Ghosting after the proposal**
Trigger: The proposal went out, the client acknowledged it warmly, and then: nothing, for two weeks and counting.
Right move: Work a planned follow up ladder, not vibes: a short nudge with one new piece of value or information at about a week, a structure question at two weeks ("has the scope or the budget moved? Either answer helps me plan"), and a clean close out message at the point your ladder ends ("I will close the file on Friday and assume the timing is wrong; the proposal stands if things change"). Then close the file and log it. Each message makes replying easier, never guiltier.
Wrong move: Escalating discounts in the dark ("just to help, we could do 10 percent off if you sign this week"), or daily check ins that teach the client your time is free and your price is theatre.

**2. Bad faith negotiation for free strategy**
Trigger: Meetings keep multiplying, questions keep deepening (architecture thinking, supplier recommendations, range strategy), but commitment keeps receding and no engagement of any size is ever quite right.
Right move: Name the pattern kindly and test it with a paid step: "We have covered a lot of the strategic ground in these calls. The next layer is the discovery phase; here is its price and what it produces." A genuine buyer buys or explains. A harvester declines and reveals the meetings were the product. Log the diagnosis.
Wrong move: Continuing to consult for free because the brand is prestigious and the meetings feel like progress. Meetings are not pipeline; signed scope is pipeline.

**3. Procurement asks for other clients' rates**
Trigger: "What do you charge your other FMCG clients?" or "Match the rate you give [named client]." Asked casually, or as a process requirement.
Right move: Refuse cleanly, once, without apology or elaboration: "Our pricing to any client is confidential, including yours. Your rates will never be quoted to another client either, and that protection is part of what you are buying." Offer what can legitimately be shared: your own rate card, your deliverables pricing, references who have agreed to be references.
Wrong move: Any disclosure, including anonymised ("a similar client pays about..."), ranges built from real clients, or confirming a number they guess. Anonymised real data is still disclosure, and it always travels.

**4. Exclusivity demand, broad and casual**
Trigger: "Obviously you would not work with our competitors" appears late, sometimes at contract stage, sometimes as a condition of a single mid sized project.
Right move: Treat as a major commercial ask, not a courtesy: escalate with perimeter (named competitors), category definition, duration, and consideration. Offer only the narrowed, time limited, compensated form if the founder approves one (playbook library entry 10). Nothing agreed in email.
Wrong move: Nodding it through verbally to keep the signing smooth, then discovering the studio has signed away a category for a project's fee. See also the scope skill's conflict logic for existing clients.

**5. Payment term creep**
Trigger: Terms agreed at 30 days become 45 in the onboarding paperwork, then "our run is actually 60" at first invoice. Each step small, each step presented as discovery rather than change.
Right move: Respond at the first movement, in writing, with the agreed terms quoted and the difference named factually. Require the agreed set (or a founder approved exception with its cost stated) before further milestones are delivered; the pause clause exists for exactly this. Creep unchallenged at step one is the terms at step three.
Wrong move: Absorbing each increment silently because the amounts feel petty. Payment terms are price; a 30 day drift on a programme is a real financing cost, and it was negotiated away from you in slices too small to fight about individually, which was the method.

**6. Legal redline storm**
Trigger: The client's lawyers return the SOW or framework with pages of redlines, including clauses far beyond the project's scale (uncapped indemnities, IP over everything, audit rights).
Right move: Separate signal from volume. Many redlines are a law firm's standard opening, not the client's commercial intent. Prepare the clause table (terms response template), mark which changes are commercially material and which are noise, escalate the material ones, and route the document to counsel with the commercial summary attached. Negotiate counsel to counsel on the legal points; keep the commercial relationship warm and separate.
Wrong move: The account executive negotiating liability language line by line by email, or the founder signing to end the discomfort. Anything signed in a storm stays signed in the calm.

**7. The buyer who negotiates twice**
Trigger: You reach agreement with the brand director; procurement then reopens every point "for process", expecting a second full negotiation.
Right move: Hold the documented agreement as the baseline: "These points were agreed with [name] on [date]; happy to document them in your format, and to discuss anything genuinely new." Give procurement their process wins that cost nothing (formats, forms, the rate card with cover note) and treat re-trading agreed substance as a new ask requiring new trades.
Wrong move: Re-negotiating from zero, which pays for the same deal twice and teaches the organisation to route every future agreement through the second bite.

**8. A verbal yes, then a cheaper signature**
Trigger: The deal is agreed at one structure; the paperwork arrives at a lower fee, a wider scope, or both, described as "admin's version".
Right move: Pause signature, diff the paperwork against the logged agreement, and return the differences factually with the log behind you: "The agreement of [date] was X; this document says Y. Happy to sign X." Most of the time this resolves as error; when it does not, you have learned something essential before the work, not after.
Wrong move: Signing and planning to "raise it later". Later has no leverage; the document is the deal.

**9. Champion goes silent, deputy appears**
Trigger: Your buyer stops replying and a more junior colleague begins asking preliminary questions, as if the negotiation is restarting.
Right move: Treat the deputy as a person, not a demotion: brief them properly, send the proposal and the agreement summary, and ask directly and kindly whether the project has changed hands or changed shape. Internally, re-diagnose: this pattern usually means the champion lost an internal fight, left, or was overruled, and the real objection is now unknown. Back to triage, not to concessions.
Wrong move: Re-pitching from scratch with fresh concessions to win the new person, which prices the studio's confusion into the deal.

**10. Deadline blackmail ("sign today or the slot goes")**
Trigger: Artificial urgency appears from either direction: the client claims another studio is waiting, or a "this week only" discount is expected of you.
Right move: Respond completely and calmly the same day, on structure. Let artificial deadlines expire; real ones (resets, launches) are already in the scope. If they say the slot will go, accept the possibility graciously: pressure that works once becomes the permanent negotiation method on that account.
Wrong move: Cutting terms to beat a clock nobody can evidence. A client who manufactures one deadline manufactures all of them.

**11. "Our CEO hates agencies / had a bad experience"**
Trigger: The objection is autobiographical: a principal with scar tissue from another supplier.
Right move: Diagnose as risk, respond with risk reducers: a paid pilot phase with a decision gate, named team, milestone structure with balance before file release protecting both sides, and references who will speak plainly. Keep it short; you cannot argue someone out of an experience, you can only make the first step small and safe.
Wrong move: Discounting. Price does not heal distrust; it usually confirms the suspicion that the number was invented.

**12. The procurement auction / reverse auction invitation**
Trigger: An e-auction platform invitation: your price per line, live, against unnamed studios, lowest wins.
Right move: Decline by default for creative work, in one warm paragraph: the studio prices deliverables, and live price auctions select for the scope misunderstandings this suite exists to prevent. Founder exception only, with eyes open about what winning an auction does to the scope conversation afterwards.
Wrong move: Participating "to see the market", then discovering that a number bid in an afternoon is on file as your rate for two years.

**13. Most favoured customer clause**
Trigger: Framework language granting the client your "best available pricing" or rates "no less favourable than any other client's".
Right move: Decline, on the confidentiality rule as much as the economics: the studio cannot warrant comparisons against terms it will not disclose, including to auditors in most forms. Offer instead a defined rate card with a review date and volume structures based on their own spend. Escalate; counsel reviews any surviving version.
Wrong move: Signing it as boilerplate. It converts every future deal you do into this client's renegotiation trigger, and it puts you in breach the first time anyone gets a better structure for legitimate reasons.

**14. Scope by spreadsheet**
Trigger: Procurement sends a workbook demanding your price be decomposed into their cells: hours per task, per pack element, per meeting, totalling exactly to your fee.
Right move: Cooperate partially and honestly: provide phase level structure and the assumptions (SKU counts, rounds), and explain that creative work is not costed per element because elements are not separable (the architecture decision prices across every SKU it organises). Offer a walk through call. What you will not do is fabricate precision: invented cell level numbers become their benchmark file and your future cage.
Wrong move: Inventing a decomposition that sums correctly and means nothing, then being held to it. Note the kinship with the scope skill's edge case on exposing rate logic: the studio's transparency model is chosen in advance, not improvised here.

**15. The incumbent is the client's relative / friend / former colleague**
Trigger: Discovery or a slip reveals the competing studio is personally connected to the decision maker.
Right move: Re-diagnose as politics and price your effort accordingly: shorten the pursuit, ask the sponsor question early ("what would have to be true for the work to move?"), and do not invest in a full pitch where the outcome is socially predetermined. Exit clean and keep the door; relationships change faster than rosters suggest.
Wrong move: Competing on price against someone's brother in law. You will lose cheaper than you needed to.

**16. Free pilot or free first SKU**
Trigger: "Do the first pack free; if it lands, the range is yours." Smaller than a full spec ask, framed as low risk.
Right move: It is spec work with a smaller hat. Offer the paid version: first SKU priced normally, or inside a paid discovery, with the range structure agreed in writing before the pilot starts (rates, volumes, decision date, what "lands" means). If the pilot terms cannot be written down, the range promise cannot be believed.
Wrong move: Accepting for the relationship and discovering the range went to tender anyway, with your pilot as the quality benchmark in the brief.

**17. Payment in product, equity, or introductions (negotiation edition)**
Trigger: Mid-negotiation, fees start converting into non cash consideration: product, shares, "access to our network".
Right move: Hold the fee conversation and the consideration conversation apart. If the founder wants the bet, it is documented separately, valued conservatively, and never nets against the invoice unless counsel papers it. Introductions are lovely and are a trade item (see playbook mechanics), not a currency.
Wrong move: Letting the fee dissolve into a basket of soft value nobody priced, discovered at invoicing.

**18. Retrospective discount demand**
Trigger: Mid-project or at final invoice: "Given how this has gone, we expect a reduction", where "how this has gone" is vague or refers to issues never raised at the time.
Right move: Ask for the specifics in writing and check them against the project record and the negotiation log. Genuine studio failures get genuine remedies, fast and generous. Vague dissatisfaction priced at invoice time gets a factual summary of deliveries and approvals, and the invoice stands. Either way, answer once, completely, with evidence.
Wrong move: An instant goodwill percentage given by phone to end the call. It sets the price of every future complaint and admits a fault that may not exist.

**19. The friendlier competitor quote that includes your scope**
Trigger: A rival quote lands in your inbox (sent by the client) and it mirrors your proposal's structure and language suspiciously closely; your thinking is being used as the tender document.
Right move: Stay gracious and stay shallow: answer on deliverables and price structure, share no further strategic detail in writing, and note the pattern in the log. If you win, the IP and concepts clauses do their work; if you lose, you have lost a document, not your method, and the follow up at the review date is a professional call, not an accusation.
Wrong move: Confronting the client about it, or enriching the shared document with more thinking to "stay ahead". The leak is the lesson; the response is containment.

**20. Multi-year rate freeze demand**
Trigger: Framework or retainer talks include "rates fixed for the term" across two or three years.
Right move: Counter with structure: a review date (annual is normal), an index linked adjustment, or a freeze in exchange for volume commitment and faster payment, with the trade explicit. A short freeze (a year) inside a good framework is tradable by the founder; an open ended one is a slow rate cut denominated in inflation.
Wrong move: Agreeing in the room to keep the signing pleasant and letting future you discover the real price of the deal in year two.

**21. Buyer asks you to coach them against their own procurement**
Trigger: The brand director, candidly: "Procurement will try to squeeze you. Here is what they did to the last studio. Help me get you through."
Right move: Accept the alliance for what is legitimate: give the buyer the internal selling kit, the deliverables framing, and honest answers to likely questions. Decline anything that involves misleading their organisation (staged quotes, false competing offers, backdated documents). Helping a buyer sell internally is the job; deceiving their employer is not, and it ends accounts when discovered, as it usually is.
Wrong move: Becoming the buyer's accomplice against their own process. Today's co-conspirator is tomorrow's witness.

**22. Sudden budget freeze mid-negotiation**
Trigger: "All spend is frozen this quarter" arrives after weeks of progress, sometimes company wide and real, sometimes a cooling device.
Right move: Believe the fact, not the finality: shrink the commitment to what survives freezes (a paid discovery, a phase 1 deposit holding the slot, a signed SOW with a future start date and kill terms that protect both sides), agree a specific reactivation date, and diary it. Log what was fully agreed so the restart begins from the agreement, not from memory.
Wrong move: Discounting to sneak under the freeze threshold. Frozen budgets are not price sensitive; they are permission sensitive, and a discount buys nothing except a permanently lower rate when the thaw comes.

**23. The negotiation that becomes a redesign**
Trigger: To reach the number, the client starts redesigning the solution itself: different format, cheaper substrate, merged SKUs, dropping the structural work.
Right move: Welcome it in the right room: that is a scoping conversation, and it belongs to the Scope and Pricing Architect, priced from the rate sheet with the production consequences stated (substrate changes carry print and quality implications the client must hear from you, not discover at proof). Pause the negotiation clock explicitly while the new scope is built.
Wrong move: Pricing the redesign live in the negotiation, from memory, to keep momentum. Live priced redesigns are how studios give away their production expertise and then honour the number.

**24. Procurement requests your financials, insurance upgrades, or audits disproportionate to the work**
Trigger: Onboarding demands audited accounts, insurance limits several times the project value, or site audits, for a modest design fee.
Right move: Price the compliance ask like any other scope item: provide what is standard and proportionate, quote what is exceptional as a cost or decline it, and escalate liability shaped items to the founder and counsel. Proportionality is a professional argument procurement itself respects; make it in those words.
Wrong move: Quietly buying insurance upgrades to "keep the onboarding moving" without anyone deciding the project pays for them.

**25. Your own team member concedes in the corridor**
Trigger: You learn that a colleague, at a client event or on a call you were not on, said "I am sure we can do something on price" or agreed a date or a term now being quoted back at you.
Right move: Fix internally first and factually: establish exactly what was said, log it, and align the team (one negotiating voice from today, agreed in those words). Externally, do not disown your colleague to the client; absorb the ambiguity as the studio's, clarify the studio's actual position once, kindly and in writing, and let the structure carry it. The client exploiting a crack is normal; the crack is the problem.
Wrong move: Telling the client your colleague "was not authorised", which advertises your internal disorder and invites them to shop for the softest voice on every future point.
