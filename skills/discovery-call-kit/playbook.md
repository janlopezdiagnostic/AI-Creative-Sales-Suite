# Discovery Call Playbook

The method behind the Discovery Call Kit: what to ask, in what order, what the answers actually tell you, and how a call becomes the brief the Scope and Pricing Architect consumes. Read this before your first prep pack; the templates assume it.

## The shape of a good discovery call

Thirty to forty-five minutes, and the buyer talks for most of it. The order matters because trust compounds: business before creative, creative before commercial, commercial before commitment.

1. **Purpose and time (2 minutes).** "Thanks for the time. My aim today is to understand what is driving this and whether we are the right studio for it. If we are, I will suggest a specific next step at the end. Is there anything you need to get from the next 40 minutes?" That last question is the highest-value sentence in the call; the answer regularly rewrites the agenda.
2. **Their world (10 minutes).** The trigger, the stakes, the deadline and what created it.
3. **The work (15 minutes).** Scope shape: deliverables, volumes, phases, production reality.
4. **The commercial frame (10 minutes).** Decision process, budget shape, who else is being considered, what happens next on their side.
5. **Close (3 minutes).** Recap what you heard in their words, name the next step, agree the date while both parties are on the call. Never end with "we will be in touch."

The seller who presents first learns last. Portfolio proof comes out when a question makes it relevant ("have you handled a 24-SKU range before?" is the buyer's invitation), in two or three matched examples, never as a deck tour.

## The question bank, by theme

Ask questions in the buyer's language. Each theme below lists the core questions, what a strong answer sounds like, and what an evasive answer tells you.

### 1. The business trigger

- "What has changed that makes this a now project?"
- "What happens if this does not happen, or happens badly?"
- "Who inside the business is feeling this most?"
- "Is this tied to a retailer conversation, a launch, or a competitor move?"

Strong answers name an event with a date: a range review, a listing at risk, a new market entry, a competitor's relaunch. Weak answers are aesthetic ("the packs feel tired"). Tired packs with no event behind them means no internal urgency, which means your proposal will sit in an inbox while urgent projects jump it. Record the trigger verbatim where possible; the brief and later the proposal both open with it.

### 2. Decision process

- "If we get to a proposal you love, what has to happen between there and a start date?"
- "Who else sees this before it is approved?"
- "Who signs at this value, and have they seen projects like this before?"
- "Has procurement got a process we should know about early, panels, onboarding, terms?"

You are mapping the buying centre: user, influencer, economic buyer, gatekeeper. Most creative deals die because the seller sold to an influencer and met the economic buyer for the first time in a negotiation. Every unnamed role is an access task in the follow-up plan, not a footnote.

### 3. Budget reality

- "How has the business thought about what this should cost: a set budget, a range, or not yet?"
- "What did the last comparable project cost, roughly?" (History is easier to share than a budget and just as informative.)
- "If our proposal came in at twice what you have in mind, what would happen?" (The reaction calibrates the gap better than any direct ask.)
- "Is the money in this year's budget, or does it need to be won?"

Never accept "we have no budget" at face value and never argue with it. No budget usually means one of three things: the project is not yet real internally, the buyer does not control money, or they are benchmarking. Each has a different tree in `decision-trees.md`. What you must leave the call knowing is the *shape*: funded and allocated, funded and contested, or unfunded and aspirational. Record exactly what was said; "budget exists" from the buyer and a number the seller guessed are different species of fact, and the brief keeps them separate.

### 4. Timeline

- "What date does this need to be finished by, and what created that date?"
- "Working backwards, when does artwork need to lock, and who controls that?"
- "What is already fixed: print slots, retailer deadlines, launch events?"
- "What happens on your side if the date moves a month?"

A deadline with a consequence is real. A deadline that is "just when we'd like it" is a preference, and the difference changes pricing, rush terms, and how hard the studio should chase. For packaging, always anchor to the physical chain: shelf date, minus distribution, minus print and production, minus artwork lock. Buyers frequently discover their own deadline is impossible while you do this arithmetic with them; better on the call than in your proposal.

### 5. Stakeholders and internal politics

- "Who will use this work day to day, and who will judge it?"
- "Who was involved in choosing the last studio, and how did that go?"
- "If your team disagrees internally about direction, how does that get resolved?"
- "Who should be on our next call so nobody has to relay?"

You are listening for the hidden veto: the founder who "doesn't get involved" but kills concepts at the end, the sales director whose retailer feedback overrides brand, the spouse, the board. Name them in the brief as roles with the access plan; never as gossip.

### 6. Creative expectations

- "Show me or describe two packs, sites, or identities you wish were yours. What is it about them?"
- "What should we never do: what would make a concept dead on arrival?"
- "How are creative decisions made here: one person's taste, committee, consumer testing?"
- "What did the last studio get wrong creatively?"

References reveal the taste ceiling and the real brief behind the brief. "Premium but accessible" means nothing; the two competitor packs they envy mean a great deal. If decisions are by committee or testing, that is a scope fact (rounds, research phases) the scoping skill needs to know now, not at proposal stage.

### 7. Production realities

For packaging this theme is not optional and it is where design-only studios lose credibility:

- "Who prints this today, and are they fixed or open?" (Flexo, litho, gravure, or digital changes what design can promise.)
- "Do dielines exist, and who owns them: you, the printer, a previous studio?"
- "How many SKUs, formats, and languages are really in play, including the ones coming next quarter?"
- "Any material or sustainability commitments already made: substrates, recyclability targets, EPR exposure in your markets?"
- "Who approves colour and signs off proofs on your side?"

Volumes drive everything downstream: SKU count, format count, and language count are the scoping skill's raw materials. Get numbers, not adjectives. If the buyer does not know, that is fine and common; record `[NOT STATED]` in the brief and make "confirm volumes" a named follow-up with an owner on their side.

### 8. Past agency experience

- "What did you value most about the last studio you worked with? What frustrated you?"
- "Why is this project not going to them?" (Asked warmly, it is the most diagnostic question in the bank.)
- "What would make you fire a studio in the first month?"

The answers are the service brief hiding inside the design brief. A buyer burned by slow responses needs a communication rhythm in the proposal; a buyer burned by surprise invoices needs the change-order mechanics explained up front. Write these into the brief's "how they like to work" field verbatim where you can.

## The question bank, by buyer type

**Brand or marketing director (the taste buyer).** Lead with trigger, creative expectations, and shelf reality. They judge whether the studio "gets it". Budget answers will be real but often bounded ("I have a range for the redesign, production is separate"). Decision process questions matter most: brand directors frequently champion but do not sign above a threshold. Ask who else sees it before they have to defend it internally, and offer the ammunition: "What would help you sell this internally?" lands well with this buyer.

**Founder (taste and budget in one person).** Shorter route, faster call, bigger traps. Founders answer budget questions directly but the number moves with their week; test it against history ("what did the identity cost at launch?"). The two founder-specific risks are free strategy fishing ("what would you do with our brand?") and the moving brief. Your job is to pin the deliverables list and the deadline driver, and to leave the creative thinking for the paid phase. Warmth yes, workshop no.

**Procurement or sourcing.** Different meeting, different rules. Procurement calls are about process: panel status, rate structures, insurance, terms, onboarding, references. Do not pitch taste to procurement and do not treat the call as discovery of the creative brief; the creative discovery happens with the taste buyer. What you extract here is the commercial frame: how studios get approved, what the evaluation criteria and weightings are, whether rates are benchmarked, and who the business sponsor is. If procurement is your *first* conversation, the deliverable of the call is a route to the sponsor, not a proposal.

## Listening forensics: what answers reveal

- **Vague trigger, specific deadline.** Usually means the deadline is inherited (a retailer's) and the project is real but the ownership is fuzzy. Find the owner.
- **"We are talking to a few studios."** Ask how many and what happens next; the answer separates a structured evaluation (fine, compete on fit) from a free-ideas harvest (eight studios, concepts requested, no budget discussed: see the trees).
- **Budget offered too early and too precisely.** Sometimes genuine, sometimes an anchor planted to shape your proposal. Record it as stated, test against history and scope size in the scorecard.
- **The buyer answers every question with a question about price.** They are benchmarking or they have a number they cannot say. Give the pricing *structure* (phases, what drives cost) and no number; a number given here becomes a ceiling with none of the scope attached.
- **Long, detailed complaints about the incumbent.** Gold for the service brief, a warning for expectations. Studios that inherit a grievance list inherit the grievance standard.
- **"Just send some rough ideas and we will know."** The spec-work fork. Rough ideas on a real brand is unpaid creative. The paid discovery route exists precisely for this moment; the tree gives the wording.
- **Silence on who signs.** Treat the deal as unqualified until access is granted, however warm the conversation feels.

## Qualification scoring

Score four dimensions 0 to 3 against the studio fact sheet (rubric in `templates/qualification-scorecard.md`):

- **Fit.** Portfolio proof matches the deliverable and category, the work is the kind the studio wants more of, no conflict.
- **Budget.** Funded and in range, funded below range, unfunded, or unstated. Unstated is not zero by default; it is unknown, and unknown blocks a full proposal until resolved or a paid discovery prices the learning.
- **Timing.** A dated driver inside two quarters scores high; "sometime next year" is nurture, whatever the fit.
- **Access.** You have spoken to, or have a dated route to, the economic buyer. Influencer-only access caps the verdict at paid discovery or nurture until it improves.

Verdicts: **pursue to proposal** (total 9 or more, no dimension at 0), **paid discovery first** (fit and timing real, budget or access unresolved), **nurture** (real account, wrong quarter), **decline politely** (fit fails, conflict, or the process itself is extractive). The seller can override a verdict only with the reason written in the brief. Warm feelings are not a reason.

## Red flags and how to surface them live

Raise flags as observations, not accusations, and raise them on the call where possible; a flag discovered in extraction with no plan to resolve it is just anxiety with formatting.

- **Eight studios, concepts requested, no budget conversation.** "So I pitch well, can I ask how you will decide between eight sets of concepts? We do our best evaluation work in a paid discovery, two weeks, fixed fee, and it is creditable. Would that fit your process?" If the answer is no and concepts are still required, the tree ends at decline.
- **Deadline inside the physical minimum.** Do the backwards arithmetic out loud, kindly. Offer the options tree: reduce scope, phase the launch, or pay rush properly. Never silently accept the date.
- **No access to the signer.** "Everything you have described needs a sign-off above this level. When we get to a proposal, can we present it to them together? I would rather hear their questions first-hand than have you carry our case." 
- **Brief contradicts the research.** The buyer describes a refresh; the trade press says full redesign. Surface it: "That is smaller than what was announced. Has the scope been cut, or is this phase one?" Either answer is valuable.
- **Free strategy fishing.** Answer one level up (principles, past analogues) and park the rest: "That is exactly what the discovery phase is for; I do not want to give you an answer today that I would not stand behind."

## Running prep when there is no time

Sometimes the call is in an hour and the research brief is two lines long. Prep anyway, shorter. The minimum viable pack is five lines: the hypothesis (even if it is a guess, labelled as one), the three must-establish questions, the two matched proof points, the price-question pivot line scripted word for word, and the planned close. Twenty honest minutes beats a skipped prep every time, because the failure modes this skill exists to prevent (ballparks, free concepts, a vague ending) all happen in unprepared calls, and all five are cheapest to prevent before the call starts. What gets cut under time pressure is background reading, never the pivot lines or the close.

## The extraction pass, field by field

Extraction is a discipline, not a summary. Work through the transcript or notes in the template's order and resist filling fields out of sequence.

**Trigger.** Find the buyer's own causal language: "because", "since", "the problem is". If the call never produced one, the field says so; a trigger assembled from the research brief alone is marked RB and flagged as unconfirmed on the call.

**Deliverables.** Harvest nouns and numbers, ruthlessly. "The range" is not a deliverable. "Nine core SKUs, two seasonal editions undecided" is. When the buyer used a number loosely ("about twenty"), record the looseness; the scoping skill would rather price a stated range with assumptions than discover the looseness in week three.

**Budget.** Read the money passages three times. First pass: what figure, if any, did the buyer actually attach to this project. Second pass: what history was offered, and is it labelled. Third pass: what did the seller say, and does the follow-up need to unwind any of it. Most extraction errors in this suite's testing live in this field, because money talk is where paraphrase most easily becomes invention.

**Decision process.** Reconstruct the chain from fragments: "Priya and I decide, Finance signs over fifty" is a chain with a threshold and a gap (the basis of "fifty"), and the extraction should present it exactly that way, gaps visible, rather than smoothing it into "Finance approves".

**What the buyer never said.** After the fields are filled, do one deliberate pass for absences: no signer named, no volumes, no mention of other studios, no production owner. Absences are findings. They become the follow-up list, and they are frequently the most commercially important part of the whole extraction, because a warm call with four absences is a paid discovery case, whatever it felt like.

## Difficult moments, handled in the room

**The long silence after a budget question.** Do not rescue it. The seller who fills a budget silence fills it with their own number. Count to ten internally; the buyer is usually deciding how honest to be, and honesty is what the silence is for.

**"What would you charge for something like this?" at minute five.** Structure, drivers, and a date for a scoped number. If pressed twice, give the studio's published phase logic ("programmes like this are priced as an architecture phase plus per-SKU artwork; the total depends on the count we are about to establish") and return to the count. A range offered "just to keep the conversation moving" moves nothing but the anchor, downward.

**The buyer starts negotiating against a proposal that does not exist.** "We could never pay X for design." Note the number and what it reveals (they have a ceiling and it has a shape), do not bargain against a hypothetical. The scorecard records it under Budget evidence, in their words.

**Tears, anger, or a founder taking it personally.** Packaging touches identity for founders; a critique of the current range can land as a critique of them. Slow down, separate the work from the person ("the range has outgrown its original system; that is a success problem"), and never use a buyer's emotional moment as leverage for a close. The brief records commercial facts only; the person's state is not data for the file.

## The brief format the scoping skill consumes

Extraction produces the creative brief in exactly the shape the Scope and Pricing Architect opens with. Fields: account and entity; trigger (buyer's words); deliverables inventory with counts (SKUs, formats, languages, phases named separately: strategy, naming, structural, artwork, production support, guidelines); timeline with the dated driver and the artwork-lock back-calculation; budget shape (funded or not, range as stated, history as stated, or `[NOT STATED]`); decision process and named stakeholders with roles; creative expectations and references; production realities (printer, processes, dielines ownership, materials commitments); how they like to work (from the past-agency theme); risks and flags; qualification scores and verdict; next step agreed, with date and owner.

Every field carries its provenance silently by construction: it was said on the call (in the notes or transcript), carried from the research brief (marked RB), or it reads `[NOT STATED]`. The scoping skill prices what is stated, flags what is not, and never has to guess which is which. That discipline, maintained across two skills, is most of what this suite is selling.
