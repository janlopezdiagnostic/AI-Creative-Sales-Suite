# Edge Cases: Account Research Brief

Twenty situations that break naive research. Each entry: the trigger, the right move, the wrong move.

## 1. The brand already has an agency of record

**Trigger:** Sources name an AOR for brand or design work.
**Right move:** Record the AOR, what scope it covers, and when it was appointed. Narrow the hypothesis to work the AOR does not cover (packaging systems, extensions, regional adaptation) or drop to watchlist until the roster is reviewed, which typically follows a leadership change or a contract cycle.
**Wrong move:** Ignoring the AOR and pitching head-on for the full account, or treating the AOR's existence as a minor detail in Risks.

## 2. An incumbent studio did the last packaging and did it well

**Trigger:** Pack credits name an incumbent, the work is current and strong.
**Right move:** Say the system is healthy. Look only for work the incumbent is unlikely to hold: new formats, new markets, speed-driven extension work. Otherwise do not pursue.
**Wrong move:** Manufacturing criticism of good work to justify outreach. Sellers get found out in the first meeting.

## 3. Procurement-led account

**Trigger:** The brand buys through a roster, panel, or tender process, or sits inside a group with a mandated agency list.
**Right move:** State the process reality in the brief, estimate the cost of playing (onboarding, tender effort, rate pressure), and recommend pursue only if the fact sheet says the studio plays procurement-led accounts, ideally with a brand-side sponsor identified.
**Wrong move:** Writing a brand-director hypothesis for an account where procurement controls entry, and letting the seller discover the tender wall after weeks of work.

## 4. Brand in stealth or pre-launch

**Trigger:** A funded startup with no public product, no packaging visible, founders quiet.
**Right move:** Brief what is knowable (category, funding, founder backgrounds, hiring signals), mark almost everything else UNVERIFIED, and set the review trigger to launch or first retail news. Stealth brands do buy design early, but through networks, so note any warm path.
**Wrong move:** Inventing a launch date, a range, or a positioning from the funding announcement. Stealth is a fact, not a gap to fill.

## 5. Conflicting signals

**Trigger:** Signals point opposite ways: expansion news alongside layoffs, a new brand lead alongside a hiring freeze.
**Right move:** Record the conflict explicitly, trust the more recent and more primary source, and let the decision trees arbitrate. Often the correct read is two different stories (one division growing, one shrinking): name which part of the business the hypothesis covers.
**Wrong move:** Averaging the conflict into a vague medium-strength brief that commits to nothing and can be quoted either way later.

## 6. Signals older than six months

**Trigger:** Every found signal is dated more than six months back.
**Right move:** Grade the stack STALE. Keep the signals in the log as context, check specifically for reactivation (a follow-up announcement, the launch actually happening), and exit as do not pursue for now with a 90-day review.
**Wrong move:** Dropping the dates from the brief so the signals look current. Recency blindness is the most common way briefs lie.

## 7. One source, repeated everywhere

**Trigger:** The same claim appears on five sites.
**Right move:** Trace it to the origin. If all five repeat one article or one press release, it is one source. Grade accordingly.
**Wrong move:** Calling it "widely reported" and upgrading it to Confirmed. Repetition is not corroboration.

## 8. The new leader is interim or acting

**Trigger:** A leadership signal turns out to be an interim appointment.
**Right move:** Downgrade the leadership signal, note that interim leaders rarely change rosters, and set the review trigger to the permanent appointment announcement, which restarts the day 30 to 150 window.
**Wrong move:** Running the standard new-leader play against someone who will not own the consequences of changing suppliers.

## 9. Parent company and subsidiary confusion

**Trigger:** News about a parent or sister brand gets attributed to the target brand.
**Right move:** Verify the entity for every signal. A parent rebrand may create portfolio work, or none at all for this brand. State the entity relationship and which entity would actually buy.
**Wrong move:** Building a brief for a brand using its parent's funding and its sister brand's launch as "its" signals.

## 10. The brand just finished a rebrand

**Trigger:** A major identity or packaging programme completed within the last 12 months.
**Right move:** Timing scores 0 for core work. Look only at rollout debt: markets not yet converted, sub-ranges left behind, production and adaptation overload. Otherwise file and review in two quarters.
**Wrong move:** Pitching a refresh of a refresh. It signals that the studio does not look before it sells.

## 11. Retailer own-brand, where the retailer is the buyer

**Trigger:** The "brand" is a retailer's own label.
**Right move:** Rewrite the stakeholder map: the buyer is the retailer's own-brand team, procurement-heavy, reset-driven, with design bought in large, structured programmes. Apply the packaging reset logic to the retailer, not the brand.
**Wrong move:** Researching it like an independent brand and looking for a brand director who does not exist.

## 12. Founder-led micro brand with no budget signals

**Trigger:** A tiny brand, founder does everything, no funding, no retail, no hires.
**Right move:** Score deal size 0 or 1 honestly. If the studio wants the relationship for strategic reasons, say so and price the pursuit accordingly (light touch, watchlist). Most of the time: polite do not pursue.
**Wrong move:** Letting beautiful work and a charismatic founder talk the scores up. Charm is not a budget.

## 13. M&A announced but not closed

**Trigger:** An acquisition is announced, regulatory or closing steps remain.
**Right move:** Treat integration brand work as post-close. Brief the current state, set the review trigger to deal close, and note that pre-close, both parties usually freeze discretionary brand spend.
**Wrong move:** Timing the hypothesis to the announcement and sending the seller in during the freeze.

## 14. Sustainability claim that is marketing only

**Trigger:** Green language on the site or in ads, no dated commitment, no material named, no report behind it.
**Right move:** Grade it UNVERIFIED as a buying trigger. Note it as positioning context only, and look for the hard version: a dated pledge, a retailer requirement, or a regulation covering the brand's formats.
**Wrong move:** Building a materials-redesign hypothesis on a slogan. If the claim is unsubstantiated, there is also reputational risk in associating; note it in Risks.

## 15. Seasonal business researched out of season

**Trigger:** A fashion, holiday, or seasonal food brand researched right after its peak cycle closed.
**Right move:** Use Tree 3's seasonal exit. Name the next buying cycle explicitly (with approximate months) and set the review to its start, not to a generic 90 days.
**Wrong move:** Scoring timing low and killing a good account, or scoring it high and pitching into a closed season. Both come from ignoring the calendar.

## 16. The target competes with a current client

**Trigger:** The conflict check finds the target is a direct competitor of a current client.
**Right move:** Stop at intake, before the sweep. Record the conflict in the log so the account is not re-proposed later, and exit Tree 2 at 0 if it is ever briefed for the record.
**Wrong move:** Researching first and arguing about the conflict later, after the studio has internal knowledge of a competitor's plans. That is a trust problem, not a sales tactic.

## 17. Distress signals: layoffs, closures, profit warnings

**Trigger:** The sweep finds contraction news rather than growth news.
**Right move:** Report it plainly and do not pursue growth work. Distressed brands cut discretionary design first. If anything, the only honest note is a long-horizon watchlist entry for post-restructuring.
**Wrong move:** Reframing distress as "they need a rebrand to turn things around" and pitching into a business that is fighting to survive. It wastes their time and the studio's.

## 18. Regulated categories (alcohol, pharma, infant food, cannabis where legal)

**Trigger:** The target sits in a category with advertising, labelling, or claims regulation.
**Right move:** Add a compliance line to the brief: what rules shape design in this category, whether the studio has shipped regulated work before (fact sheet), and a portfolio match cap if it has not. Regulation also creates real triggers (labelling changes), so date and source any rule change like any other signal.
**Wrong move:** Scoring match purely on aesthetics and discovering at pitch stage that the studio cannot evidence regulated-category experience the buyer requires.

## 19. Multi-region brand with different incumbents per region

**Trigger:** The brand operates in several markets with different studios or in-house teams per region.
**Right move:** Scope the brief to one region: the one with the signals. Name the region in every claim. A global hypothesis built on one region's news is an error.
**Wrong move:** Writing a single global brief from regional signals and letting the seller pitch headquarters about a decision made in a regional office.

## 20. The user asks you to fill gaps from "what usually happens"

**Trigger:** Input is thin and the user pushes: "just assume typical budgets and decision makers for a brand this size."
**Right move:** Decline the assumption, mark the fields unknown, and either run the brief with explicit unknowns and capped confidence, or pause for the clarifying questions in SKILL.md. Offer the provisional brief format instead.
**Wrong move:** Complying. A brief full of typicals is fiction with tables, and it will be quoted back as research.
