# Playbook: Account Research for Creative Services

This playbook is the method behind the Account Research Brief skill. It covers the signal taxonomy (what counts as a creative buying trigger and where to find it), the packaging layer, fit scoring, hypothesis writing, stakeholder mapping, and the anti-patterns that turn research into spam.

## The method at a glance

Creative work is bought on triggers, not on budgets sitting idle. A brand with no change underway almost never buys a redesign, no matter how good the portfolio is. A brand in motion, a new leader, a new line, a new retailer, a new material rule, buys quickly and often buys from the first credible studio that connects the change to relevant proof. Research therefore starts with change, not with the company description. The workflow is: sweep for signals, verify and date them, score the studio's right to win, map who decides, then write one hypothesis that ties a signal to proof inside a time window. If any link in that chain is missing, the honest output is watchlist or do not pursue.

## Signal taxonomy

Each signal below is described the same way: what it is, why it creates creative buying, where it is found, what makes it strong or weak, and the typical window in which the buying happens.

### 1. Rebrand or identity change

**What:** The brand announces or visibly begins an identity change: new logo appearing in market, a brand architecture review, an agency search reported in trade press, or a quiet redesign rolling out unevenly across touchpoints.
**Why it buys:** Identity work cascades. A rebrand pulls packaging, web, campaign, and retail materials behind it, usually over 6 to 18 months.
**Where found:** Trade press, the brand's own press page, design press coverage, trademark filings showing new marks, job posts for brand design leadership, conference talks by the CMO.
**Strong:** An announced programme with a named leader or a visible rollout already inconsistent across touchpoints (a sure sign of work in progress and work remaining). **Weak:** A rumour, a single refreshed social avatar, a logo tweak with no rollout.
**Window:** Early. The biggest scopes are set in the first two quarters. Late arrivals compete for leftovers.

### 2. New CMO, brand lead, or creative director

**What:** A senior marketing or brand hire, especially from outside the company.
**Why it buys:** New leaders review agencies and suppliers early, bring preferred partners, and need visible wins. This is the single most reliable trigger in services selling. (The "first 90 days" window used across this suite is a rule of thumb from practitioner practice, not a measured constant; treat it as a prioritisation guide, not a deadline.)
**Where found:** Company announcements, trade press people moves, LinkedIn profile changes (supplied by the user, manual mode), conference speaker bios.
**Strong:** External hire at VP level or above with a mandate that mentions brand, growth, or transformation; a leader who has bought creative services before. **Weak:** Internal promotion with no mandate change; an interim or acting appointment (note it, but expect slow decisions); a hire into a purely performance-marketing remit.
**Window:** Days 14 to 90 after start (rule of thumb, as above). In the first two weeks they are still listening; after roughly day 90 the roster is usually set.

### 3. Product line extension

**What:** The brand launches products adjacent to its core: a sauce brand adding dips, a skincare brand adding body care, a beverage brand adding a functional line.
**Why it buys:** Extensions need packaging that stretches the existing system without breaking it, launch materials, and often a naming and architecture decision. Incumbent studios do not always win these; extension work is frequently briefed separately because it is fast.
**Where found:** Trade press, retailer range reviews, trademark filings for new product names, the brand's own launch announcements, distributor catalogues.
**Strong:** A named launch with a date, a retailer attached, or filings covering several product names (a pipeline, not a one-off). **Weak:** A teaser with no product, no date, no channel.
**Window:** Packaging decisions usually land 4 to 9 months before shelf date. Finding the signal at announcement often means the design is already done; filings and retailer news earlier in the chain are worth more.

### 4. SKU proliferation

**What:** The count of variants grows: new flavours, sizes, formats, limited editions, localised versions.
**Why it buys:** Each new SKU is small individually and large in aggregate. Systems strain: hierarchy gets muddy, production artwork multiplies, and someone eventually briefs a range rationalisation or a design system cleanup.
**Where found:** The brand's online store and retailer listings over time, range announcements, packaging credits, production artwork job posts.
**Strong:** Rapid growth (a range visibly expanding within a year), proliferation across formats that do not share a system, seasonal or limited editions layered on top. **Weak:** Steady, managed variant growth inside a clearly disciplined system; that brand has its house in order.
**Window:** Slow burn. This signal supports a systems conversation rather than a project conversation, and pairs well with a refresh-cycle signal.

### 5. Funding or M&A

**What:** A raise, an acquisition, being acquired, or a merger.
**Why it buys:** Funded brands spend on brand to justify valuation and fuel distribution gains. Acquired brands get rebranded, integrated, or repositioned by the new owner. Acquirers harmonise portfolios, which creates large, unglamorous, well-funded design programmes.
**Where found:** Business press, funding databases, company announcements, regulatory filings for public companies.
**Strong:** Growth-stage funding with stated retail or geographic expansion; an acquisition where the buyer has a history of portfolio rebrands. **Weak:** A small raise with no expansion story; an acquisition explicitly framed as "no changes, business as usual" (believe it for at least two quarters).
**Window:** Brand spend typically lands one to three quarters after the money. M&A integration work follows the deal close, not the announcement.

### 6. Retailer reset or new retail placement

**What:** The brand wins new distribution, enters a new retailer, or faces a category reset at an existing one.
**Why it buys:** New retail means new shelf context, new pack size or format requirements, retailer-specific compliance, and often a packaging review because the pack now sits next to different competitors. Resets force every brand in the set to justify its place, and tired packaging is an easy reason to lose facings.
**Where found:** Retail and grocery trade press, retailer range announcements, the brand's distribution news, category management coverage.
**Strong:** A named retailer, a date, and a category known for design-led resets; a move from direct-to-consumer into physical retail for the first time (the pack was designed for a screen and now has three seconds on a shelf). **Weak:** Distribution growth with no date or unnamed "selected stores".
**Window:** Tight and unforgiving. Packaging work must finish ahead of reset production deadlines, so the buying window opens as soon as the placement is real and closes fast.

### 7. Sustainability pledge or material change

**What:** The brand commits to recyclable, compostable, refillable, or reduced packaging; or a rule change forces material changes: extended producer responsibility schemes, plastic taxes, labelling rules, retailer sustainability scorecards.
**Why it buys:** Material changes are design changes. A new substrate changes print, structure, colour, and shelf impact, and somebody has to redesign the range to survive it. Pledges with dates create programmes; pledges without dates create press releases.
**Where found:** Sustainability reports, company announcements, trade press, regulatory updates for the brand's markets, retailer supplier requirements.
**Strong:** A pledge with a deadline and a named material, or a regulation with a compliance date that covers the brand's formats. **Weak:** Aspirational language with no date, no material, no baseline. Treat as reputation management, not a buying trigger, until a harder signal appears.
**Window:** Long programmes, often 12 to 36 months, with design briefed early because testing and compliance take time.

### 8. Packaging refresh cycle

**What:** No announcement at all. The packaging is simply old. Most consumer brands refresh core packaging every 3 to 5 years; some categories move faster.
**Why it buys:** Even without news, age plus drift (inconsistent variants, dated photography, competitors who have already refreshed) predicts a brief within a year or two.
**Where found:** The pack itself: copyright dates, design credits, archived versions in web archives, the competitive set on shelf today.
**Strong:** Packaging visibly older than the category norm, competitors refreshed within the last year, and any second signal from this taxonomy on top. **Weak:** Age alone, with a disciplined system and no other change. Age alone earns a watchlist entry, not an outreach.
**Window:** Unknown by definition. This signal sets the review cadence; other signals set the action date.

## The packaging layer

Packaging accounts get four extra checks, because packaging buying follows production and retail logic that general brand research misses.

**Retailer reset calendar.** Major grocery and mass retailers reset categories on cycles, commonly spring and autumn planogram reviews, with brand submissions and artwork deadlines months earlier. When researching a packaged brand, identify its main retailers, find the reset rhythm for its category if it is published or reported, and work backwards: artwork deadlines minus production time minus design time equals the last realistic date to win the work. A brief written after that date is archaeology. Record reset dates in the signal log even when they are approximate, marked as such.

**Dieline and SKU structure changes.** Structural change is the deepest packaging trigger. New formats (a pouch replacing a carton, a family size, a club-store format), new dielines, or a range moving to a new structure all force full redesign of the affected SKUs and often a system review. Watch for format launches, pack size changes in retailer listings, and packaging engineer or structural design job posts, which often appear before any public news.

**Sustainability and material rules.** Packaging is where sustainability pledges become engineering. Check three layers: the brand's own commitments with dates; retailer requirements (several major retailers score or mandate recyclability and recycled content for own-brand and increasingly for national brands); and regulation in the brand's markets, including extended producer responsibility fees that rise for hard-to-recycle formats, labelling rules, and restrictions on specific materials. Regulation dates are hard deadlines and the most fundable programmes in packaging. Always record which layer a material signal comes from; a regulatory deadline outranks a marketing pledge.

**Print and production hints.** Co-packer changes, printer consolidation, moves between print processes, and entry into markets with different print standards all surface as design adaptation work. These signals are rarely announced; they appear in operations job posts, supplier news, and trade press. They indicate adaptation and rollout work rather than concept work, which suits some studios and bores others. Say which it is in the brief.

## Where signals are found: source map

Work sources in this order of trust: primary sources (the brand's own announcements, filings, reports, job posts) first; trade press for the brand's category second; business press for funding and leadership third; aggregators and databases (enrichment tools, funding databases) fourth, as leads to verify rather than as evidence; social posts last and weakest. A signal from layer four or five must be promoted by verification before it can carry a brief. Every signal in the log carries its source layer so the reader can judge the brief at a glance.

## Scoring fit

Score three dimensions from 0 to 3. Use the fact sheet, not enthusiasm.

**Portfolio match.** 3: the studio has shipped the same deliverable in the same or an adjacent category, with named proof it can show. 2: same deliverable, different category, or same category, adjacent deliverable. 1: the studio could plausibly do the work but has nothing to show. 0: the work sits outside the studio's services or in a category the studio excludes. A score of 0 ends the brief: do not pursue, regardless of signal strength. Strong demand does not create capability.

**Deal size fit.** Estimate the likely project from the signal: an extension of 6 SKUs is not a brand transformation programme. 3: squarely inside the studio's typical range. 2: at the edge of the range, or unclear but plausibly inside. 1: probably below the floor but strategic (a foot in a portfolio brand, a category the studio is deliberately entering). 0: clearly below the floor with no strategic case. Mark the estimate as an estimate; deal size from outside is always a range, never a number dressed up as fact.

**Timing.** 3: the buying window is open now (signal dated within the last quarter and its window, per the taxonomy, is current). 2: the window opens this quarter or next. 1: the window is seasonal or later in the year; set a review date tied to the trigger that would open it. 0: the window has closed (launch already in market with design finished, roster already announced) or cannot be placed at all.

**Reading the total.** 7 to 9: pursue now. 4 to 6: watchlist with a specific review trigger, unless portfolio match is 3 and timing is 3, in which case pursue with a narrow, signal-specific angle. 0 to 3: do not pursue. Any single score of 0 forces its tree exit in `decision-trees.md` regardless of total.

## From signals to an opportunity hypothesis

A hypothesis is not a summary. It is a falsifiable claim in one or two sentences with four parts: the trigger, the likely need, the time window, and the studio's right to win. Formula: "Because [dated signal], [brand] will likely need [specific work] by [window]. [Studio] can credibly win it because [named proof], and the conversation should start with [stakeholder role]."

Tests a good hypothesis passes: it names work specific enough to scope (not "brand support" but "packaging system extension for a 6-SKU dip range across two formats"); it would be proven wrong by findable facts (the launch slips, the work goes to the incumbent, the leader leaves); and it survives the removal of its weakest signal. If deleting one unverified signal kills the hypothesis, the brief must say so and drop to watchlist.

State confidence plainly: high (confirmed signals, clear window, proof in category), medium (confirmed signal but window or proof is partial), low (rests on reported or unverified material). Low confidence hypotheses do not go to outreach. They go to the watchlist with the verification step that would raise them.

## Stakeholder mapping

Creative purchases involve three centres of gravity. Map all three, even when one is unknown.

**Brand director / head of brand.** Owns the outcome and usually the shortlist. Cares about: does this studio understand the category and the consumer, is the proof relevant, will this make the brand stronger and make them look good internally. They sign off on creative direction and usually champion the budget. Research should establish their tenure (new in role is itself a signal), their background (agency side or brand side), and the brands they have run before.

**Procurement.** Owns the process once spend passes a threshold. Cares about: rate cards, comparability, risk, insurance, payment terms, and whether the studio can survive onboarding. Procurement rarely picks the creative winner but frequently kills the unprepared. A procurement-led account is not a bad account; it is an account where the brief must note the process reality (roster, panel, tender) and the studio must decide whether it plays that game. See the edge cases file for procurement-led accounts.

**Founder / CEO.** In founder-led and smaller brands, the founder is the brand director, the budget, and the taste, all in one. Cares about: conviction, speed, and whether the studio "gets it". Founder-led deals close fast and die fast. Research should note founder visibility (they often state brand ambitions publicly, which is a legitimate research source) and whether a professional brand lead has been hired underneath them, which usually signals the shift from taste-led to process-led buying.

Also note, when findable: who ran the last comparable project (the incumbent is a stakeholder even in absence), and whether a parent company sets a roster that overrides local choice. A brand inside a group with a mandated agency roster is a different pursuit entirely.

## Combining signals: stacks beat singles

Single signals mislead. A sustainability pledge alone is often marketing. A new brand lead alone may inherit a locked roster. The briefs that convert come from stacks: two or more independent signals pointing at the same work in the same window. Weight stacks explicitly. A line extension (signal 3) plus a retailer reset in the same category (signal 6) plus dated packaging older than the category norm (signal 8) is a strong stack: the brand must produce new packaging, to a deadline, starting from an ageing system. A funding announcement (signal 5) plus a first retail placement (signal 6) is the classic DTC-to-retail stack: money arriving exactly when the pack must be rebuilt for shelf. Conversely, watch for stacks that cancel: an acquisition (signal 5) plus "no changes planned" statements plus a group roster announcement is a stack pointing away from opportunity, and the brief should say so. When signals conflict rather than stack, do not average them into a mushy medium. Record the conflict, name which signal you trust more and why (usually the more recent, more primary one), and let the decision trees arbitrate.

## Anti-patterns

**Generic firmographic spam.** "50 to 200 employees, consumer goods, North America" is not research. If the brief could be about any company in the category by swapping the name, it is worthless. Every section must contain at least one fact that could only be true of this brand.

**Signal laundering.** Repeating a claim from an aggregator until it sounds confirmed. Sources do not multiply by being copied. One origin, however many repetitions, is one source.

**Recency blindness.** Treating a year-old announcement as current. Date everything; downgrade or discard what is stale.

**Portfolio wishful thinking.** Scoring match on work the studio wishes it had. The fact sheet separates proven categories from target categories precisely to prevent this.

**Hypothesis stacking.** Listing five possible needs so one is bound to be right. One hypothesis, clearly stated, with its confidence. Alternatives belong in one short "other angles" line, not as co-equal claims.

**Research as delay.** A brief that takes a week serves no one. The sweep is timeboxed: if the sources are not producing dated signals within the session, the correct output is a short brief that says so.

## Cadence and review

Every brief carries a review date. Pursue-now briefs are reviewed when outreach has run its course. Watchlist briefs are reviewed on the earlier of: the trigger event named in the brief, the next retailer reset for the category, a leadership change, or 90 days. Do-not-pursue briefs are reviewed in two quarters, briefly, to confirm nothing structural changed. Research is a living log, not a pile of one-off documents; the signal log is the memory that makes each subsequent brief faster and more honest than the last.
