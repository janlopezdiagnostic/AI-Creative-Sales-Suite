# Playbook: Revival and Referral for Creative Services

This playbook is the method behind the Revive and Referral skill. It covers stall diagnosis, trigger-based revival, the value-add touch library, breakup emails, the multi-quarter long game, and the referral engine: satisfaction peaks, ask structures, effortlessness, reciprocity, and tracking. The standing rules from SKILL.md apply everywhere below. Two of them shape everything in this document: opt-outs are permanent, and nothing about a relationship may be invented.

## The method at a glance

A stalled deal is not a dead deal, and a lost deal is not always a closed one. But revival only works when something has changed. The discipline is: diagnose why it stopped, name the change that would unblock it, wait for real evidence of that change (a sourced, dated trigger), and then reappear carrying value, not neediness. Referrals follow the same shape: satisfaction is the trigger, the ask is the touch, and effortlessness is what converts goodwill into action. In both engines, the amateur move is volume. The professional move is timing plus specificity.

## Part 1: Stall diagnosis

You cannot revive what you have not diagnosed. Stalled or lost creative services deals usually come back to one of five causes. Each has a different unblock condition, and therefore a different play.

### No decision (the silent majority)

**What happened:** The proposal landed, meetings were warm, then nothing. No competitor won; the project simply lost internal priority, the approval chain stalled, or the client went quiet rather than saying no. In creative services this is the most common stall and the most revivable, because the need was real and nobody chose an alternative.
**Tells:** No rejection was ever stated. Replies slowed rather than stopped abruptly. The last exchange was logistical ("circulating internally") rather than substantive.
**Unblock condition:** A forcing event that raises the project's internal priority (a launch date, a retailer deadline, a new leader asking questions), or a lower-friction way back in (a smaller first phase, a paid discovery step).
**Revival posture:** Patient, useful, periodic. Value-add touches on real triggers. A breakup email after a defined sequence closes the loop cleanly and often produces the answer the nudges never did.

### Lost to incumbent

**What happened:** The work went to the studio or agency already in place, or the client renewed with a known quantity. This is a loss, not a stall, and it should be recorded as one. Incumbency is a rational choice under deadline pressure; it is not a verdict on your work forever.
**Tells:** A decision was communicated. The stated reason is usually safety, speed, or an existing relationship rather than price or quality.
**Unblock condition:** Incumbent failure or capacity strain (visible in market: late launches, visible quality drops, the incumbent losing staff), a scope the incumbent does not cover (new format, new market, extension work), or a roster review cycle.
**Revival posture:** The long game, measured in quarters. Gracious at the loss, occasionally useful, never bitter and never critical of the incumbent's work in writing. You are positioning to be the obvious call when the situation moves.

### Budget frozen

**What happened:** The project was approved in spirit and killed by money: a budget freeze, a bad quarter, a finance hold, a round of cuts. The client often tells you this plainly and apologetically.
**Tells:** A stated financial reason, a named revisit date ("next cycle", "after Q2"), an invitation to stay in touch that sounds genuine because it is.
**Unblock condition:** The budget cycle turning: a new fiscal year, a new quarter after results, a funding event, a category that starts performing again.
**Revival posture:** Believe the date they gave you. Set the return for just before the planning window of the cycle they named, arrive with the scope re-cut to the budget reality you now know, and do not burn the relationship with monthly check-ins in between that communicate you did not believe them.

### Champion gone

**What happened:** Your internal sponsor left the company, moved roles, or lost influence, and the project had no second owner. The deal did not die; it was orphaned.
**Tells:** The champion's departure is confirmed (announcement, profile change supplied by the user, an out-of-office or a bounce). Remaining contacts are polite and noncommittal because the project was never theirs.
**Unblock condition:** A successor arriving (the single highest-value trigger in this whole playbook), or the champion resurfacing at a new company where they again own relevant budget, subject to conflict checks at the new account.
**Revival posture:** When a successor is named, the play is a clean introduction that makes the predecessor's work easy to inherit: a short summary of where things stood, no complaints about the stall, and the proposal attached as history rather than pressure. When the champion resurfaces elsewhere, treat it as a new account with a warm path, run a proper research brief first, and check category conflicts at the new employer before any contact.

### Timing wrong

**What happened:** The need is real but the window is not now: mid-contract with another supplier, mid-rebrand already underway, a seasonal window already missed, a launch calendar with no slot. The client often says "not this year" and means it precisely.
**Tells:** A concrete reason tied to a calendar or a contract. Frequently the client names the future window themselves.
**Unblock condition:** The named window arriving. Nothing else. Attempting to accelerate a genuine timing stall reads as not listening.
**Revival posture:** Agree the date, log it, send at most one genuinely useful thing in the interval if a strong trigger appears, and return shortly before the window opens with evidence you remembered what they said.

### Diagnosis discipline

Assign one primary cause per deal in the tracker, even when several contribute; the primary cause chooses the play. Write the evidence for the diagnosis (the client's words, the dates) next to it. If the record supports no diagnosis, the deal is not ready for revival activity; it needs history recovery first, usually a short internal review of email and CRM notes, not a message to the client guessing at what happened.

## Part 2: Trigger-based revival

A trigger is a sourced, dated event that changes the blocking condition from Part 1. Grade triggers exactly as Skill 1 grades signals: confirmed (primary source or two independent sources), reported (one credible source), or unverified. Revival touches may be built on confirmed and reported triggers, with the grade reflected in how assertively the touch is written. Unverified triggers justify watching, never sending.

**Brand news.** Launches, line extensions, new retail placements, rebrands surfacing in market, awards, funding, M&A. Strong when it creates the exact work that was stalled (a line extension announced after a packaging project froze is close to a written invitation). Weak when it is generic good news with no creative implication ("nice funding round, anyway about our proposal" is not a touch, it is a non sequitur).

**Leadership change.** A new CMO, head of brand, or creative director at the account. The highest-velocity trigger for champion-gone and no-decision stalls. The new leader reviews suppliers and inherits open questions; arriving in their first weeks with a concise history of the stalled conversation positions the studio as helpful context rather than another cold pitch. Apply Skill 1's window discipline (the first 90 days, strongest roughly days 14 to 90, as a rule of thumb).

**Seasonal windows.** For packaging and brand work: retailer resets (commonly spring and autumn planogram changes, with artwork lock 8 to 12 weeks before shelf date, verified per retailer), seasonal ranges (work starts two to three quarters before it shelves), annual planning cycles, and category review calendars. A stall diagnosed as timing becomes revivable on a date you can compute, and the tracker should carry that date the day the deal stalls, not the day someone remembers it.

**Competitor moves.** A direct competitor of the client rebranding, launching, or winning distribution raises the internal cost of standing still. Use carefully: the touch shares a market observation and its implication, never gloating and never confidential information about any party. If the competitor is also a studio client, the conflict rules apply and the trigger is unusable.

**Regulatory and market clocks.** In packaging especially: EPR and PPWR obligations, recyclability labelling, claims rules, deposit schemes. A compliance deadline converts a discretionary redesign into a mandatory one with a date. Verify the specific rule and date for the client's market before citing either, exactly as the packaging layer requires.

## Part 3: The value-add touch library

A revival touch must carry something the recipient can use even if they never reply. If the touch would be worthless to them in the (likely) event the deal stays dead, it is a nudge, not a touch. The library, strongest first:

**The teardown.** A short, specific, genuinely useful critique of something current and public: their range architecture on a retailer site, shelf standout against two named competitors, hierarchy problems introduced by a recent SKU addition. Two or three observations, one implication, no pitch. Rules: critique only what is public, never disparage the incumbent who made it (they may be reading over the client's shoulder, and the client chose them), and never manufacture flaws. A teardown that invents problems destroys the credibility it was meant to build.

**The trend note.** Three to five lines tying a category movement to their specific range: a substrate shift, a format trend, a labelling change, with the source and date. Short enough to read in under a minute. The value is the relevance filter, not the information, which they could find themselves.

**The relevant case result, with permission.** "The range refresh we shipped for [client, with their permission to cite] lifted [outcome the client has acknowledged publicly or in writing]." Only results the cited client has approved for external use, stated exactly as approved. An unapproved result, an exaggerated one, or a result from a competitor of the recipient (conflict) ends careers in a small industry. When in doubt, describe the work type without numbers.

**The introduction or resource.** Connecting the client to someone useful to them (a production specialist, a retailer contact where appropriate), or sending a genuinely relevant resource with one line on why it is relevant to their situation specifically. Generosity with no hook is remembered disproportionately, because so little of what lands in their inbox is free of one.

**Cadence for touches.** One touch per genuine trigger, or roughly one per quarter in the long game, whichever is less frequent. Two touches with no reply and no new trigger means the next move is a breakup email or silent parking, not a third touch.

## Part 4: Breakup emails that get replies

The breakup email is a real close, not a bluff. It works because it gives the recipient a low-cost way to answer and removes the low-grade guilt of the unanswered thread, which is what most stalls actually are.

**Structure:** One line referencing the real history (project name, last real exchange, its approximate date, from the record, never invented). One line stating the assumption you are making ("I will assume the timing is wrong and close this out on my side"). One line leaving the door genuinely open with the lowest-friction next step. One line of thanks with no sarcasm. Four lines is a ceiling, not a target.

**Rules:** Send it once. If it gets no reply, the deal is parked or closed and the record says so; a second breakup email converts dignity into a bit. Never use the breakup format as a first or second contact. Never fake a breakup to manufacture urgency on a deal you intend to keep chasing monthly. And never send one to anyone whose stall involved a stated no, a conflict, or an opt-out; those are already closed or permanently closed, and the email would be exactly the contact the rules forbid.

## Part 5: The multi-quarter long game

Serious studios run revival as a portfolio, not as a series of improvisations. The tracker holds every parked deal with: stall cause, last contact date, permission status, the named trigger(s) that would revive it, and the next review date. Review the tracker monthly: any triggers fired? Any computed windows (reset dates, budget cycles, leadership changes) arriving in the next 30 days? Any records whose trigger has gone stale, which move to closed with the reasoning written down. A deal with no plausible trigger and no review date is not in the long game; it is clutter, and clutter is how opt-outs and conflicts get missed later. Expect the maths to be humble: most parked deals never revive, and the engine earns its keep on the minority that do, plus the referrals that a reputation for graceful follow-through generates.

## Part 6: The referral engine

### Satisfaction peaks, and how to detect them

Referrals are asked for at satisfaction peaks, and peaks are detectable if you are looking at evidence rather than mood. The reliable peak moments:

- **Launch day and the days just after,** when the work goes live, ships to shelf, or is announced, and the client is showing it to their own organisation.
- **The results moment,** when the client shares an outcome: distribution won, a retailer compliment, sell-through, an internal award. Their words about the result are the strongest evidence in the whole engine.
- **The unsolicited praise moment,** an email or message the client sends that says, in their own words, that working with the studio was good. Save the exact words in the satisfaction log (with their knowledge where the channel implies privacy). This is evidence and, later, testimonial raw material they have already half-written.
- **The repeat-buy moment.** A client who returns with a second project has demonstrated satisfaction behaviourally. The ask can ride on the kickoff goodwill of the new project.
- **The rescue moment,** after the studio fixed a problem fast and well. Handled correctly, a problem recovered well can leave a client more loyal, not less; the ask comes after the fix is confirmed good, never during the fire.

Detection discipline: the account lead flags peaks into the satisfaction log as they happen. Peaks decay; a launch high is worth acting on within two to three weeks. Praise older than a year, or praise followed by a later problem that was never resolved, is not a peak and does not support an ask.

### The ask structures, lightest to heaviest

Ask for one thing at a time, matched to the strength of the evidence and the relationship.

1. **Review.** A rating and a few words on a platform the studio actually uses in its sales process. Lowest effort for the giver, useful social proof. Provide the direct link; never script the text of a review beyond offering topics.
2. **Testimonial.** A short quote, attributed with name, title, and company (each attribution element needs its own permission). Draft from the client's own recorded words where they exist, show them the draft, and make editing or declining effortless. Never publish a word they did not approve in its final form.
3. **Case study permission.** The right to tell the story of the work: scope, process, and outcomes. Specify exactly what would be shown (images, results, quotes) and where. Permission for the portfolio is not permission for results claims; those are separate approvals.
4. **Warm introduction.** The heaviest ask and the most valuable. Name the specific person or company you would like to meet and why the introduction is genuinely good for them too, or at least neutral. Never ask for "anyone who might need design"; a vague ask makes the giver do your targeting and quietly taxes the relationship.

### Making it effortless for the giver

Every ask ships with the work already done: the draft testimonial in their words awaiting a yes or an edit, the two-sentence forwardable introduction blurb they can copy, the direct review link, the named target and the one-line reason. Provide a graceful out in the same message ("if now is not a good time, no follow-up from me on this"). Then honour the out absolutely: no second nudge on a declined or ignored ask. An ignored referral ask is answered; record it and wait at least two quarters and a fresh peak before any new ask, if ever.

### Reciprocity and tracking

Referral goodwill runs both ways. Where there is a genuine fit, the studio refers back: introductions, work it cannot take, suppliers, candidates. Reciprocity is never transactional per-referral and never a condition stated to the client; it is a posture, logged like everything else. Every ask, answer, introduction, and outcome is logged in the tracker and the CRM: who was asked, for what, when, the answer, and if an introduction landed, the resulting pipeline entry, its research brief, and its outcome months later. This log is what lets the studio know its real referral rate, thank people accurately, and never double-ask.

### What never to do

- **No kickbacks or undisclosed incentives.** Paying or gifting a client personally for introductions breaches most corporate policies and some laws, and if it needs concealing from their employer it is already wrong. A public, uniform thank-you (a note, a modest courtesy within the giver's policy) is the ceiling.
- **Never ask during a problem.** Not during a delay, a complaint, a budget dispute, or an invoice chase. The ask contaminates both conversations.
- **Never inflate.** Do not ask the client to say things stronger than their recorded words, do not edit a testimonial into a different claim, and do not use a logo whose permission is only implied.
- **Never burn the introduction.** If a referred prospect is not a fit, decline fast and graciously and tell the giver what happened. The giver's reputation travelled with that introduction; the report back is the rent you pay on it, and it is what earns the second one.
- **Never ask twice in a row.** One ask per peak. Serial asking converts goodwill into avoidance, and avoidance is how studios lose the client, not just the referral.

## Part 7: Tone, in both engines

Every output of this skill is read by a person who once chose, or nearly chose, to work with the studio, and who will judge the studio by how it behaves when it is not winning. Three tone rules govern every draft. First, brevity is respect: the recipient owes you nothing, so every sentence must earn its place, and the whole touch should be readable in under a minute. Second, confidence without need: state what you have (a trigger, an observation, an offer) plainly and stop; qualifying, over-explaining, and apologising for writing all signal that the sender does not believe the message deserved to arrive. Third, warmth that survives a no: write every touch so that if the answer is no, or nothing, the recipient's opinion of the studio is unchanged or slightly improved. If a draft only reads well when it succeeds, it is not ready to send.
