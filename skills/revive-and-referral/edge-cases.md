# Edge Cases: Revive and Referral

Twenty situations that break naive follow-up. Each entry: the trigger, the right move, the wrong move. The standing rules from SKILL.md (opt-outs permanent, nothing invented, triggers sourced and dated) apply to every entry, including the ones that do not restate them.

## 1. The champion moved to a competitor's client

**Trigger:** Your departed sponsor now works at a brand that competes directly with a current studio client.
**Right move:** Run the conflict check before anything else. If the new employer is a category conflict, there is no warm outreach, full stop; log it and close the path. If there is no conflict, treat the new account as new: Skill 1 brief first, then a warm-path approach that references only the real prior working relationship.
**Wrong move:** Drafting a "great news about your new role, we should talk" message on the strength of the relationship alone, and discovering the conflict after the client does.

## 2. The client was merged or acquired

**Trigger:** A parked account is acquired; brand, budget, and decision makers are absorbed into a parent organisation.
**Right move:** Rebuild the picture before any contact: who owns brand decisions now, whether the acquirer has a roster or AOR (check for a genuine conflict or an incumbent wall), and whether the brand survives at all. Grade all pre-acquisition history as context, not as live relationship capital.
**Wrong move:** Reviving against the old org chart and addressing people whose roles no longer exist, or pitching the acquirer as if the old stall never happened.

## 3. A contact explicitly opted out, but a colleague at the account has not

**Trigger:** One person unsubscribed or said do not contact; another person at the same brand is in a stalled thread.
**Right move:** The opt-out binds to the person absolutely and is never routed around. A genuinely separate conversation with the colleague, about their own remit, with no reference to the opted-out person and no attempt to reach them through the colleague, may proceed under Tree 4 if the record supports it. When in doubt, treat the account as closed and ask the user to make the call consciously.
**Wrong move:** Using the colleague as a side door to the opted-out contact, or "cc'ing them just in case".

## 4. The referral giver's introduction goes badly

**Trigger:** The introduced prospect is rude, ghosts after the giver vouched for you, or the first meeting collapses.
**Right move:** Protect the giver first. Thank them promptly, report back with the facts kept kind ("timing was not right on their side"), absorb any awkwardness yourself, and never make the giver feel their judgement is on trial. Their willingness to introduce again depends entirely on this report back.
**Wrong move:** Complaining to the giver about the prospect's behaviour, going silent because the outcome is embarrassing, or billing the bad meeting to the relationship by asking for a replacement introduction.

## 5. A revival reveals the work went in-house and failed

**Trigger:** A touch lands and the client admits the project was done internally and the result is poor (packs underperforming, a launch that had to be pulled, internal team overwhelmed).
**Right move:** Respond to the problem, not the victory. No "we did warn you" in any form. Offer a bounded, practical first step (a paid diagnostic, a systems review) that helps them recover, and let the scope conversation grow from usefulness.
**Wrong move:** Any trace of gloating or retrospective salesmanship about the original proposal. The client remembers exactly who was gracious in the worst month of their year.

## 6. The prospect replies warmly but the budget is still zero

**Trigger:** A value-add touch gets a genuine, friendly reply that ends with "still no budget, sadly".
**Right move:** Believe them, thank them, and convert the exchange into tracker intelligence: updated stall cause (budget frozen), likely cycle, named review date. One graceful touch that positions for the next cycle beats a negotiation against a budget that does not exist.
**Wrong move:** Treating warmth as an opening to negotiate scope against zero, or escalating touch frequency because "they like us".

## 7. The champion left and the successor is the incumbent's ally

**Trigger:** The new decision maker arrived from, or is known to favour, the incumbent studio that beat you.
**Right move:** Slow down. Skip the early-window introduction play; instead log the account in the long game, wait for a scope the incumbent does not cover or a roster review, and keep any eventual approach narrow and proof-led.
**Wrong move:** Racing to brief against the incumbent in the successor's first month and confirming every prior they brought with them.

## 8. The client asks you to keep chasing them

**Trigger:** "We are hopeless at replying, please do keep nudging us, we do want this."
**Right move:** Take the permission seriously and structure it: agree a cadence and a date in writing ("I will check in at the reset decision point in March"), log it as an explicit standing permission with its scope, and follow it exactly.
**Wrong move:** Treating the remark as a lifetime licence for unlimited contact, or ignoring it and going silent out of generic cadence discipline when the client has genuinely asked otherwise.

## 9. A testimonial arrives that overclaims

**Trigger:** A happy client sends praise that exaggerates ("they doubled our sales") beyond anything the work can be shown to have caused.
**Right move:** Publish only what can be supported. Go back warmly with a lightly edited version trimmed to what is defensible, ask them to approve the edit, and keep the original exuberance for morale, not marketing.
**Wrong move:** Publishing the overclaim because the client wrote it. Their sentence, your liability, and the industry will check.

## 10. The referred prospect is a competitor of the giver

**Trigger:** A client's introduction target competes with the giver, or with another studio client.
**Right move:** Surface it to the giver before accepting the introduction ("you may not have realised they sit opposite your sister brand"), and run the conflict check on your own client list too. Let the giver decide with full information; decline the introduction if either conflict is real.
**Wrong move:** Quietly taking the introduction and letting two clients discover the overlap themselves.

## 11. The client went quiet because of a failure on your side

**Trigger:** The "stall" is actually an unresolved delivery problem, a billing dispute, or a quality complaint the seller has not mentioned.
**Right move:** Stop the revival framing entirely. This is service recovery, owned by the delivery side: acknowledge, fix, and confirm. Referral eligibility resets only after a fresh, post-fix satisfaction peak. Record the real stall cause so the tracker stops lying.
**Wrong move:** Drafting a cheerful value-add touch over an open complaint and converting a recoverable problem into a lost client.

## 12. Procurement resurfaces the deal as a rate exercise

**Trigger:** After a stall, procurement invites the studio onto a panel or asks for rates "to reopen the conversation".
**Right move:** Name the reality: this is a procurement process, not a revival of the creative conversation. Follow the packaging layer's discipline (never let procurement be the first conversation): seek the brand-side sponsor, price participation honestly, and decide from the fact sheet whether the studio plays panels at all.
**Wrong move:** Treating the rate request as buying intent and competing on day rate for work that was originally scoped on value.

## 13. A past client publicly criticises the category or agencies in general

**Trigger:** Your referral candidate posts or publishes complaints about agencies, fees, or a bad experience that may or may not be about you.
**Right move:** Read carefully and honestly. If it could be about the studio's work, it is evidence against satisfaction: no ask, and consider a genuine, non-defensive check-in about their experience as a delivery matter, not a sales one. If it clearly concerns others, still downgrade the timing; nobody refers well mid-rant.
**Wrong move:** Asking for a testimonial in the same week and letting them screenshot the two side by side.

## 14. The trigger is the client's crisis, and the studio genuinely could help

**Trigger:** A recall, a forced label change, or a regulatory order means the client urgently needs exactly the studio's packaging expertise.
**Right move:** Slow, human, and specific: one message, after the immediate emergency, offering concrete help (surge artwork capacity, regulatory re-artwork experience) with no reference to the stalled commercial history and no pitch structure. If you would not send it to a friend in that week, do not send it. When the motive is the contract rather than the help, Tree 2 says do not use.
**Wrong move:** The "perfect moment to revisit our proposal" framing in any wording. The industry is small and this sentence is permanent.

## 15. The deal stalls because the client contact is job-hunting

**Trigger:** Signals (supplied by the user, manual mode only) suggest your contact is interviewing; their silence is personal, not commercial.
**Right move:** Do nothing commercial with this information. Maintain normal, kind contact at the agreed cadence. If they land somewhere relevant, edge case 1 discipline applies at the new account: conflict check and a proper brief first.
**Wrong move:** Confronting them, accelerating touches to "get in before they leave", or sharing the speculation anywhere, including in the tracker as fact rather than unverified context.

## 16. The giver asks for a referral fee

**Trigger:** A client offers introductions but asks, openly, for a percentage or a gift in return.
**Right move:** Decline the arrangement plainly and without moralising: the studio does not pay for referrals from client-side contacts: it would breach their employer's policies in most cases, and it breaches this studio's own stated policy, which is why the policy is written down in the fact sheet before any ask is made. Keep the door open to an unpressured introduction with no strings, and log the conversation factually.
**Wrong move:** Agreeing "just between us", or counter-negotiating the percentage. There is no compliant version of a secret per-deal payment to an employee of the buyer.

## 17. A logo permission exists but the client changed hands

**Trigger:** The brand whose logo and quote you publish was acquired; the new owner's brand team is reviewing all endorsements.
**Right move:** Re-confirm permission with the new owner before the next use, and pause prominent use until they answer. Permissions belong to the entity that granted them and do not automatically survive its sale.
**Wrong move:** Treating the old sign-off as permanent and letting the acquirer's lawyers be the ones to tell you otherwise.

## 18. The stalled deal's contact is now at the studio's dream account, but the dream account is mid-tender

**Trigger:** A known champion arrives at a target account that is running a formal procurement process.
**Right move:** The process outranks the relationship. The champion may be contacted only as the process allows, with no request for inside help, scoring insight, or preference. Log the tender dates and let the brief and the bid do the work.
**Wrong move:** Asking the champion to "put in a word" inside a live tender. That endangers their job and your eligibility in one move.

## 19. Two people at the studio are reviving the same account

**Trigger:** A founder and an account executive both have touches planned to the same parked account in the same month, neither aware of the other.
**Right move:** The tracker is the single source of truth: one owner per account, touches logged before they are sent, and the monthly review checks for duplicates explicitly. Merge the efforts into one touch by the owner with the stronger record.
**Wrong move:** Letting both send. Double contact reads as disorganisation and halves the credibility of each.

## 20. The referral worked, and now the giver expects ongoing preferential treatment

**Trigger:** A referring client begins to expect discounts, priority scheduling, or scope favours "after all I have sent your way".
**Right move:** Thank them genuinely and hold the commercial line warmly: reciprocity in kind (introductions back, first look at insights, honest priority in conversation) rather than in price or queue-jumping that harms other clients. If expectations escalate, have the conversation early and explicitly.
**Wrong move:** Quietly conceding rate or priority and building a referral engine whose hidden cost is margin on the giver's own account.
