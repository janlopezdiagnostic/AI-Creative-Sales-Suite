# Example 4: Refusing Spec Work Gracefully, and Winning the Meeting

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative. The paid discovery figure comes from a fictional scope output.

## Inputs

- Situation: Solstice Drinks Co. (fictional), seasonal sparkling drinks, 14 SKUs and growing, heading into a spring retail reset. Discovery went well. Then the email: "Before we commit, our team would like to see two concept routes for the spring range, just rough, so we can align internally. Every studio we are talking to is doing this."
- Scope output: a paid discovery offer exists (3,550, fixed, two weeks: shelf and competitor audit, range architecture, scoped plan; fee credited in full against the full project if it proceeds within 60 days), and the full redesign tiers behind it.
- Library: a drinks line extension case (named, outcome citable), a seasonal beverage case (imagery only), full process artifacts from a completed audit.

## The response the skill drafted (for the user to send)

Subject: Solstice spring range, next step

[Name],

Fair question, and I will give you a straight answer rather than a polished one. We do not do unpaid concept routes, including rough ones, for two reasons that are both about your project rather than our policy.

First, a route designed before the architecture is settled is a guess with good rendering. Your range's actual problem, from what we saw in discovery, is that fourteen SKUs read as fourteen products. A concept would decorate that problem, not solve it, and you would be judging studios on decoration.

Second, rough routes have a way of becoming the project. They get carried internally, adapted, and built on, and the thinking that made them work never gets paid for or finished properly.

Here is what we will do instead, and it tests the thing you actually need to judge:

1. In Thursday's meeting, we will walk you through the seasonal range we redesigned last year, with the architecture decisions narrated and the shelf photographs, and you can interrogate how we think for as long as you like.
2. We will show you the audit method and the range architecture work from a completed project, redacted where our client requires it.
3. If you want routes for Solstice itself, that is precisely what the discovery phase is: audit, architecture, and the scoped plan, fixed at 3,550, two weeks, and the fee is credited in full if the project proceeds within 60 days. Routes live inside it, properly, with the architecture underneath them.

If another studio shows you routes this week, ask them what the architecture costs. That answer will tell you a lot.

Thursday still works our side. [Sign-off]

## What happened in the meeting (fictional outcome)

The client kept the meeting. The narrated case ran 40 minutes because the brand manager kept stopping it to compare decisions with her own range. The audit artifacts answered the "can they think" question the concept request had really been asking. Discovery was approved at 3,550 the following week, at the scope output's figure, with the credit term as written.

## Critique

Strengths: the refusal gives the client's underlying need a better instrument, twice (narrated work, then paid discovery), and explains the studio's reasons as the client's risks, not the studio's rules. The one competitive line is about a question to ask rivals, not a claim about them, so it survives the incumbent-in-the-room test. Note what the draft never contains: a Solstice concept, a moodboard for Solstice, or a "direction we might take" sketch.
Watch outs: tone is doing heavy lifting; this draft works because discovery was genuinely warm. Against a formal RFP the same position must be stated once, plainly, inside their structure (see edge case 10), not argued in a letter. And if the user had overridden and demanded routes anyway, the skill records the override under Assumptions with pitch cost stated; it does not quietly become a concept machine.
