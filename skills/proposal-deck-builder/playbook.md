# The Proposal and Deck Playbook

This is the method behind the skill. Read it once end to end. After that, the templates and decision trees carry the day to day weight.

The core belief: creative proposals are not won by the studio with the most pages or the prettiest template. They are won by the document that makes the client's actual problem feel understood, proves with finished work that the studio has solved that problem before, and makes saying yes feel administratively easy. Everything else, including beautiful typography, is support. A proposal is a sales instrument with a legal shadow, not a portfolio with a price taped to the back.

## Part 1. Proposal anatomy, section by section

The standard proposal document runs nine sections. Small jobs compress them (see the one-page proposal template); nothing below Better-tier work omits sections 1, 5, 7, or 9.

### 1. Understanding (the mirror)
Restate the client's problem in their own language, drawn from the discovery brief: the business situation, the deadline pressure, the internal stakes, what failure looks like. Two to four paragraphs, no studio claims yet. This section exists because clients approve people who demonstrably listened. It is built entirely from discovery material; if you cannot write it without inventing, discovery was too thin and the skill should say so.

Rule: mirror, do not flatter. "Your range is invisible at three feet in a fixture dominated by two national brands" wins. "Your exciting brand" loses, silently, with every experienced buyer.

### 2. Insight (the reframe)
The one thing the studio sees that reframes the problem: a range architecture flaw, a shelf-blocking opportunity, a claims hierarchy problem, a regulatory clock the client has not priced in. One page maximum. The insight comes from category observation and past work, and it must be true and sourced from material the studio actually holds: an audit, shelf photography, the client's own data from discovery. An insight that requires designing the client's new pack to express is spec work wearing a trench coat; cut it back to the observation.

### 3. Approach (how, not what)
Describe the method: phases, what happens in each, what the client sees and approves at each gate, who from the client side is needed and when. This is where process artifacts earn their place: a redacted audit spread, an architecture diagram from a finished project, a workshop photograph. Approach answers the buyer's real question, which is never "are they talented" but "what will working with them actually be like, and where can it go wrong".

Name the risks the studio manages explicitly: SKU count drift, late regulatory copy, dieline changes, committee feedback. A studio that names the failure modes in its own proposal looks like the adult in the process, because it is.

### 4. Relevant work (the proof)
Two to four cases, selected and placed per Part 4 of this playbook. Each case gets the same discipline: the problem in one line, what the studio did in two or three lines, the deliverables, and the outcome, but only where the library records the client's permission to cite it. Where permission does not exist, the case runs on imagery and deliverables alone, and the text says nothing about results. Never average, blend, or borrow outcomes across cases.

### 5. Scope (what is bought)
Transcribed from the Scope and Pricing Architect output: tiers, deliverables per tier with quantities (SKUs, rounds, formats), exclusions in plain language, assumptions. Do not paraphrase scope into warmer language; paraphrase is where quantities quietly change. If a client-facing summary is needed for the deck, it is labelled a summary and the document section governs.

### 6. Team (who does it)
The named people who will actually do the work, with the role each plays on this project and the one past project that makes them credible for it. See Part 5. No stock team page recycled across proposals; buyers can tell, and a team page that names people who will never touch the job is a claim the studio cannot substantiate.

### 7. Process and timeline
Phases against the client's real calendar: the retailer reset, the launch date, the artwork lock the calendar implies. Show client-side obligations (consolidated feedback within the stated window, copy and regulatory sign-off dates) as milestones too. A timeline that only shows studio work is fiction; the delays that kill packaging projects are client-side and supplier-side, and the honest page says so. Full detail in Part 6.

### 8. Price
The tiers from the scope output, presented per Part 7. Price is a section of the document, not a surprise at the end of a call, unless the price placement tree put it in the meeting instead.

### 9. Terms summary and next step
A plain-language summary of the commercial terms from the scope output: deposit, milestones, revision caps, kill fee by stage, change order rule, source file position. It is a summary, it says so, and the SOW is the binding document. Flag the section as drafting aid for counsel review. Then the next step, singular: what the client does, by when, and what happens immediately after (deposit invoice, kickoff date held). A proposal that ends with "let us know your thoughts" has no next step and usually gets none.

## Part 2. Narrative arcs by deal type

The arc is the order in which the reader's mind changes. Build pages to the arc, not to a template's habits.

### Range redesign or refresh (the flagship)
Arc: *The shelf moved, your range did not, here is the evidence, here is the studio that has fixed exactly this, here is the calm way it gets done before the reset.*
Beats: (1) category change, evidenced from the audit; (2) the cost of the current range's invisibility or confusion, stated carefully from client-supplied data only; (3) the architecture insight; (4) the closest redesign case, before and after; (5) approach and gates; (6) timeline against the reset date; (7) tiers and terms. The emotional register is steadiness. Redesign buyers are usually spending other people's money under deadline; sell the absence of drama.

### New brand or NPD launch
Arc: *This launch gets one first impression, the window is fixed, here is how the studio de-risks a thing that has never existed.*
Beats: (1) the launch window and what fixes it (retail listing, trade show, season); (2) the unknowns, named honestly (unformulated products, unsettled naming), and which of them the scope prices and which it defers; (3) a launch case, ideally one that shipped on a hard date; (4) approach with decision gates sequenced against the window; (5) the phased scope (launch SKUs now, adaptation rate locked for followers); (6) price and terms. Founders read this arc; write founder-direct, short sentences, no agency fog.

### Line extension
Arc: *Your range works, the extension is where ranges usually get diluted, here is the architecture discipline that prevents it, and it is fast because the system already exists.*
Beats: (1) why extensions fail (dilution, architecture drift) with an example from past work; (2) the base architecture fee and per-SKU logic, explained once, clearly; (3) the timeline, which is the real selling point; (4) tiers sized by SKU count. Keep it short. A line extension proposal over ten pages signals a studio that will be slow at the work too.

### Agency of record / retainer pitch
Arc: *You do not need a project, you need the range to stop being a series of emergencies; here is the guardianship model and the people who will hold it.*
Beats: (1) the client's pattern of work over a year, assembled from discovery, showing peaks, rushes, and wasted motion; (2) the cost of the current pattern, only from client-supplied numbers; (3) the retainer structure (from the scope output): pool, overage, review rhythm; (4) the two or three cases that prove range stewardship, not one-off brilliance; (5) governance: who talks to whom, response times the studio actually commits to; (6) the quarterly review mechanism; (7) terms and the exit position, stated plainly, because AOR buyers are testing whether the studio fears the exit conversation. Confidence here is quiet and operational.

### Fashion and seasonal work
Arc: *The season does not move; here is a studio that thinks in your calendar.*
Beats: (1) the season's fixed points (market week, lookbook deadline, retail delivery) and the working-backwards plan; (2) one seasonal case showing calendar discipline; (3) the deliverables by drop or collection moment; (4) tiers. Fashion buyers judge the document itself as a sample of taste; restraint in the document is the credential. See the fashion example in this module.

## Part 3. Deck design direction

A deck is a presented argument. Every page must survive two tests: it works read aloud by the presenter, and it works read cold by the person it gets forwarded to. Pages that only work with the presenter narrating are marked as such in the outline, and the leave-behind proposal carries their content in prose.

### Structure rules
- One idea per page. If a page needs the word "and" in its headline to cover its content, it is two pages.
- Headlines are claims, not labels. "The range loses at three feet" is a headline. "Market overview" is a label and tells the reader nothing. If the claim cannot be substantiated from the brief or the library, the headline comes down to what can be.
- Sequence: mirror, insight, proof, approach, team, timeline, price (where the placement tree puts it), ask. Do not open with the studio's history; nobody has ever bought a studio because it was founded in a year.
- Length discipline: 12 to 18 pages for a standard proposal deck, 6 to 10 for a line extension or small job, up to 25 for an AOR pitch with governance detail. Page count beyond that is usually unselected evidence; cut cases before cutting words.

### Visual direction (how to brief the slides)
The skill briefs slides; it does not design them. Brief in whatever tool the studio uses (Figma Slides, Google Slides, Keynote) with these directions:
- Image-led pages: one large image per page for proof sections, real project photography or pack shots from the library, never stock imagery of other people's packaging, never renders of the client's product (that is spec work).
- Type hierarchy: headline set large and short (six to ten words), one supporting line beneath it, detail pushed to speaker notes or the proposal document. If body copy is needed on a page, cap it around 40 words; beyond that the page belongs in the document.
- Show the work at honest scale: pack shots at shelf context where the library has them, architecture diagrams full-bleed, process artifacts (dielines, audit spreads) shown as evidence, slightly unpolished on purpose; polish belongs to finished work, credibility belongs to process.
- Consistency: one grid, one type system, the studio's own identity applied with restraint. The deck is a sample of the studio's judgement; a chaotic deck from a design studio is a self-own no narrative survives.
- Accessibility and forwarding reality: export a PDF for sending; check that headlines survive a phone screen, because that is where the forwarded deck gets read.

## Part 4. Case study placement

Selection beats volume. The rules:

1. Two to four cases per proposal. More reads as insecurity and dilutes the one that matters.
2. Order by rhyme, not by fame. The case whose problem most resembles this client's problem goes first and gets the most space, even if a bigger brand sits in the library. Buyers pattern-match to their own situation, not to logos.
3. Each case states: problem, intervention, deliverables, outcome (permitted only). If the library entry lacks a permitted outcome, the case is evidence of capability, and the text must not smuggle a result in through adjectives ("hugely successful", "transformative") any more than through numbers.
4. Place proof adjacent to the claim it supports. The redesign case sits inside the redesign arc after the insight, not in a lump at the back. Credentials and logo walls, where used at all, go in the credentials one-pager, not mid-argument.
5. Retire stale cases. Work older than the library's own relevance window, or work by people who have left, gets flagged to the user rather than silently reused.

## Part 5. Team pages

- Name the people who will do this job: the lead, the designer or designers, the production hand, the account contact. Role on this project first, biography second, one line each.
- Attach one relevant past project to each name. "Led the 24-SKU redesign for [library case]" is a credential. A paragraph of career history is not.
- Founders appear where founders actually work. If the founder sells and vanishes, the team page that implies otherwise is an unsubstantiated claim with a headshot.
- Never credit anyone with work from a previous employer unless the library records it as personally theirs and presentable. When in doubt, the self-check fails it and the line comes out.
- Photographs: consistent, current, human. Skip photographs rather than use a five-year-old conference shot of someone who has left.

## Part 6. Process and timeline pages

- Build the timeline from the client's fixed points backwards: shelf date or launch, artwork lock (typically 8 to 12 weeks before shelf for retailer work, verify per retailer), print and production windows, approval gates, strategy and design phases.
- Show three lanes: studio work, client obligations, supplier or third-party dependencies. Client obligations get dates and owners (consolidated feedback within the window the scope states, copy freeze, regulatory sign-off). This page is a commercial instrument disguised as a diagram: when the project later slips on a late client input, this page is the evidence the conversation was had early and kindly.
- Mark the critical path and the single most likely delay, with the mitigation. Buyers trust a studio that has visibly thought about what goes wrong.
- Seasonal and retailer calendars come from the vertical layer; a timeline that ignores the reset cycle it claims to serve fails the self-check.

## Part 7. Pricing presentation

The numbers come from the Scope and Pricing Architect, transcribed exactly. Presentation is about order and framing, never alteration.

- Show tiers, not a single number, wherever the scope output has tiers: Good, Better, Best side by side, Better marked recommended, differences expressed as deliverables, rounds, and support, never as quality adjectives.
- Put the scope beside the price, always. A price page with no deliverables on it invites the reader to compare it against the cheapest number they have ever heard.
- State what is not included on the same spread as the price, in the same size of type as other body text. Buried exclusions convert a won deal into a dispute; see the scope skill's standing rules.
- Payment shape appears with the price: deposit, milestones, when the balance falls due. Clients do not object to deposits they saw early; they object to deposits that arrive as a surprise in the SOW.
- Placement: run the price placement tree (decision-trees.md). Document proposal: price sits after approach and proof, before terms. Presented deck: price appears after the approach has landed, never on page two, and the presenter reaches it before the final third of the meeting so questions have somewhere to go. Never email a naked number ahead of the document unless the tree's early branch was deliberately chosen.
- If the scope output still carries [TO CONFIRM] items, the price page carries them too, visibly. A proposal that hides its own open items will be found out at the SOW stage, at maximum embarrassment.

## Part 8. Terms summary

- Summarise, do not improvise: deposit and milestones, revision caps and the price of extra rounds, kill fee by stage, change order rule, source file and usage position. Every line traces to the scope output.
- Label it a summary, state that the SOW governs, and flag it: "Drafting aid, have counsel review before use."
- Keep it to one page in the document, three lines in the deck. Terms that run longer than the approach section signal a studio that expects the relationship to go wrong.
- Never soften terms in the proposal that the SOW will state harder. The summary and the SOW must be reconcilable line by line.

## Part 9. The anti-spec-work toolkit

When a client wants to see "what you would do with our brand" before paying, the studio has better things to show. In ascending order of commitment:

1. **Relevant finished work, annotated.** Past packs on screen with the thinking narrated: why the architecture is built that way, what the shelf demanded. This demonstrates exactly the faculty the client is trying to test, on work that is already paid for and permitted.
2. **Process artifacts.** The audit method, a range architecture diagram from a completed project, the dieline and pre-press discipline, workshop outputs. Clients who ask for concepts are usually asking "can these people think"; artifacts answer that question directly.
3. **Category thinking, given away.** The studio's read of the client's shelf, competitors, and constraint set, from discovery. Insight is cheap to give and expensive to fake, and it designs nothing.
4. **A credentials conversation with the people who did the work.** Reference calls with past clients, where the library and the client permit. A reference call is worth more than a route, and it cannot be shopped to a rival.
5. **Paid discovery.** The priced first phase: audit, architecture, and the scoped plan, with routes and concepts inside it where the scope puts them. This is the destination of the alternatives ladder (decision-trees.md). Frame it on its merits: the client is buying the work that makes every later number accurate, and it is theirs to keep.

What never goes on the ladder: "just one quick concept", an unpaid route, a render of the client's product, naming candidates, or a moodboard that is a concept in a false moustache (imagery selected to pre-design this client's pack is spec work; imagery showing the studio's past world is credentials).

## Part 10. RFP response structure

RFPs invert the proposal: the client's structure governs, and creativity lives inside compliance.

1. **Qualify first.** Run the comply or qualify-out tree (decision-trees.md) before writing a word. An RFP the studio cannot win or cannot profitably serve is declined early and politely; the decline is also marketing.
2. **Mirror their structure exactly.** Their sections, their order, their numbering, their page limits. Evaluators score against a rubric; a beautiful restructure they cannot map to the rubric scores zero in the sections it skipped.
3. **Answer the question asked, then add the one proof.** Each answer: direct response, then the single most relevant library case or artifact. Not three. Evaluators read dozens of responses.
4. **Write the spec creative demand through the ladder.** If the RFP demands concepts, respond with the strongest compliant alternative the rules allow (past work, process, paid discovery where the framework permits a priced phase), state the studio's position once, plainly, without lecturing the evaluator. If concepts are a pass/fail gate and the studio's policy is no, that is a qualify-out fact, surfaced to the user before effort is spent.
5. **Price in their format, from the scope output.** If their format forces a single number where the scope has tiers, the recommended tier is the number and the tier structure is explained in the permitted narrative space. Never invent a blended rate to fit a cell.
6. **Compliance hygiene.** Named files as instructed, deadlines in their timezone, every requested declaration and certificate actually attached, version numbers on documents. More RFPs are lost to administration than to quality.
7. **Terms exceptions, stated.** Where the client's framework terms conflict with the studio's (payment days, IP assignment, source files), list the exceptions in the section provided, plainly. Hoping procurement will not notice is not a strategy; it is a deferred argument.
