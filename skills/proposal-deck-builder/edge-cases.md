# Edge Cases

Format: trigger, the right move, the wrong move. Prices and terms always come from the Scope and Pricing Architect output, never from this file. Every case below assumes the spec work rule and the standing rules still apply.

**1. The proposal gets forwarded to procurement only**
Trigger: The taste buyer forwards the document to procurement, who engage on price and terms without ever seeing the work presented.
Right move: Ask the user to request a short working session with procurement plus the sponsor in the room, and prepare a procurement annex: rate card logic, tier structure rationale, compliance and insurance facts from the studio's records. The sponsor must stay in the conversation; offer to brief them with the two-minute version of the argument.
Wrong move: Negotiating the full deal with procurement alone over email, or re-sending the same document with a discount pencilled in.

**2. The deck gets presented by someone else**
Trigger: The client contact offers to "present it internally" for the studio, or a partner agency will walk the board through it.
Right move: Build the cold-read version deliberately: headlines that state the claims in full, speaker notes converted to on-page supporting lines, the price and next step stated in the document, and a one-page summary the presenter can read verbatim. Ask for the meeting's attendee list so the outline weights the right concerns.
Wrong move: Sending the presented deck as-is and hoping the narration travels with it.

**3. The client asks for the deck before the meeting**
Trigger: "Send the deck ahead so we can review internally" before the scheduled presentation.
Right move: Send the proposal document (built to be read cold) and hold the deck for the room, explaining once, plainly: the deck is a presented walkthrough and reads thin without the conversation; the document carries the full argument. If the client insists, send the cold-read deck version, never the speaker-note version with internal commentary.
Wrong move: Refusing outright and looking evasive, or sending the working deck with speaker notes, draft pages, and internal debate still in it.

**4. Version control chaos**
Trigger: Multiple proposal versions circulating (v3, v3-final, v3-final-JS-edits), the client quoting an older price or scope.
Right move: One canonical file, dated and versioned in the filename and on the cover page, a change note stating what changed and that this version supersedes all earlier ones, and old links or attachments withdrawn where the tool allows. The cover email names the canonical version explicitly.
Wrong move: Quietly editing the shared file after sending, or letting two versions with different prices both stand.

**5. A claim the studio cannot substantiate**
Trigger: The user wants an outcome, award, ranking, or capability in the draft that is not in the case library and has no source ("just say the redesign lifted sales").
Right move: Refuse the specific claim, keep the case on what is sourced (deliverables, the work itself, the permitted facts), mark the gap [TO CONFIRM] with a note naming the source that would settle it (client permission, the award listing, the report). Offer the honest sentence that carries most of the weight.
Wrong move: Publishing the claim because the client "probably will not check", or laundering it into an adjective ("transformative results") to avoid a number.

**6. The incumbent is in the room**
Trigger: The client's existing studio or agency of record will see the proposal or attend the presentation.
Right move: Write and present as if they are reading, because they are: no disparagement, differentiation on fit and evidence only, process strengths stated as facts about the studio, never as comparisons the studio cannot source.
Wrong move: Building the argument on the incumbent's failures, real or rumoured. It reads as insecurity and usually reaches the incumbent verbatim.

**7. The client asks the studio to present to their board or leadership**
Trigger: A short-notice invitation to present up a level, often with a different audience mix (finance, operations).
Right move: Re-weight the existing deck, do not rebuild it: outcomes and risk first, calendar and commercial shape early, creative proof compressed to the strongest case, price placement re-run through Tree 3 for a finance-inclusive room. Confirm who chairs and what decision the meeting can actually take.
Wrong move: Presenting the sponsor's deck unchanged to a board that cares about different questions, or treating it as a bigger version of the same meeting.

**8. The sponsor goes quiet after delivery**
Trigger: Document sent, meeting held, then silence past the agreed decision date.
Right move: One structured follow-up drafted for the user (a short note restating the next step and the date, plus one new piece of value: a relevant signal, a calendar warning such as the reset clock). Then hand the account to the Revive and Referral skill's rhythm. This skill drafts the first follow-up only.
Wrong move: A drip of anxious nudges, a revised proposal with a spontaneous discount, or re-sending the document "in case it got lost" three times.

**9. The client shares the proposal with a competing studio**
Trigger: Evidence or suspicion that the document is being used to brief or benchmark a rival.
Right move: Note it, tighten what future documents carry (thinking and evidence, never buildable direction), and let the user decide whether to raise it. The anti-spec discipline in this skill is precisely the protection: a shopped proposal should contain nothing a rival can execute from.
Wrong move: Accusing the client without evidence, or withdrawing in a way that burns a relationship over what may be normal internal circulation.

**10. The RFP demands concepts as a scored section**
Trigger: Concepts are requested and scored, but not a pass/fail gate.
Right move: Run Tree 4, then Tree 2. Respond in their structure with the strongest ladder alternative their format permits (annotated past work and process evidence in the concepts section, with the paid discovery position stated once), and flag the scoring risk honestly to the user: this section may score low and the rest must carry it.
Wrong move: Submitting free routes "because everyone else will", or leaving the section blank without a stated position.

**11. Price was held for the meeting, and the meeting is cancelled**
Trigger: Tree 3 Exit B was chosen; the client cancels and asks for everything in writing instead.
Right move: The proposal document now carries the price in its normal position, with a cover email that says plainly the price section is included since the meeting moved. Do not let a held price drift in email fragments.
Wrong move: Sending the deck with the price page still missing, or quoting the number in a chat thread with no scope attached.

**12. The client wants the proposal in their template**
Trigger: A procurement or brand template arrives with fixed fields, word counts, and a rate table format.
Right move: Comply with the format, keep the argument: mirror their fields, put the tier logic in whatever narrative space exists, and keep a studio-format copy as the canonical internal version. Rate table cells are filled from the scope output only; a cell that demands a blended or unit rate the scope does not contain gets the closest honest entry plus a clarifying note, never an invented figure.
Wrong move: Refusing the template and sending the studio's prettier document, or inventing rates to fit their grid.

**13. Two decision makers want different documents**
Trigger: The brand director wants the creative argument; the founder or finance lead wants one page with numbers.
Right move: One canonical proposal, two front doors: the full document, plus a one-page summary (tiers, timeline, next step) extracted from it, generated from the same source so the numbers cannot drift. Both cover emails name both artefacts.
Wrong move: Maintaining two separately edited documents until their prices disagree in the client's inbox.

**14. The library case's client has changed hands or objected**
Trigger: A featured case's client has been acquired, rebranded, or has withdrawn enthusiasm for being showcased.
Right move: Check the library entry's permission note before every use, not once. If permission is stale or unclear, the case runs imagery-only with no name and no outcome, or comes out; flag it to the user to re-confirm with that client.
Wrong move: Assuming a permission given for a 2023 credentials deck covers a 2026 proposal to that client's direct competitor.

**15. The timeline in the proposal is already impossible**
Trigger: Drafting reveals the client's deadline (a reset, a launch) cannot be met from today's date at standard pace.
Right move: Stop and surface it before the document does. Options drafted for the user: phased scope (launch SKUs first), the rush structure from the scope output if it exists, or an honest revised date with the calendar evidence. The proposal must never carry a date the studio knows it will miss.
Wrong move: Writing the client's date into the timeline page and hoping production absorbs it.

**16. The user wants a competitor's proposal structure copied**
Trigger: The user has a rival's proposal (from a shared client, a leak, a former employer) and wants the same structure or wording.
Right move: Refuse the copying. Publicly knowable structure (section order, approximate length, what evaluators expect) may be benchmarked; the document is then built original from the studio's own evidence. Also flag the provenance problem: a document a client shared in confidence is that client's confidential material.
Wrong move: Paraphrasing their text, reusing their section inventions, or treating the leak as research.

**17. A stakeholder joins late and reopens settled scope**
Trigger: After the proposal is sent, a new voice (a founder, a regional lead) questions deliverables or price agreed in discovery.
Right move: Draft a short addendum response for the user: restate the discovery basis, point to the proposal section that carries it, and offer a call. Do not edit the canonical proposal mid-decision unless the user instructs a new version, which then follows the version rules in case 4.
Wrong move: Rewriting and resending the whole document for each new stakeholder until nobody knows which version is live.

**18. The deck must work in another language or market**
Trigger: The proposal goes to a parent company or a regional team in another language.
Right move: Flag every claim, idiom, and regulatory reference for local review; keep figures and timelines in the client's units and date formats; recommend the translation be checked by a native speaker on the client side or a professional, and record who checked it. Category vocabulary from the vertical layer does not translate itself.
Wrong move: Machine-translating the final document and sending it unchecked, especially the terms summary.

**19. The client asks for the working files of the proposal itself**
Trigger: "Send us the deck source so we can adapt it internally."
Right move: The PDF is the deliverable. Source files of the studio's own sales materials follow the same logic as client source files: they are the studio's working system. The user may choose to share; the skill's draft offers the PDF plus any specific assets the user names.
Wrong move: Sending the editable master with the full case library, speaker notes, and internal versions inside it by default.

**20. The win is announced but the terms drift at contracting**
Trigger: The client accepts, then the SOW or their purchase terms arrive with different payment days, IP assignment, or revision language than the proposal's terms summary.
Right move: Produce a line-by-line reconciliation for the user: proposal summary vs their document, each difference named, each marked accept, negotiate, or escalate to counsel. The terms summary in the proposal exists precisely so this comparison is mechanical. Nothing is signed off inside this skill; the user and counsel own that.
Wrong move: Letting the discrepancy pass because the deal feels won, or re-trading the whole negotiation in the contracting email.
