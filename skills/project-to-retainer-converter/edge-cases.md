# Edge Cases: Project to Retainer Converter

Each entry: trigger, right move, wrong move. Rates, where mentioned, always come from the studio rate sheet; nothing here authorises inventing one.

1. **Champion leaves the client mid-retainer.**
   Right move: Treat the retainer as needing re-earning. Send the successor the results recap and the usage record within weeks of their arrival, offer a short introductory review, and expect the next quarterly review to function as a re-pitch. Keep delivering flawlessly in the gap; the work is the argument.
   Wrong move: Relying on the contract term and the departed champion's enthusiasm, and meeting the successor for the first time at renewal.

2. **Procurement resets all suppliers.**
   Right move: Comply professionally and early: rate card from the sheet, insurance and compliance documents, references from permitted case material. Brief the champion so the business case (recap evidence) reaches the room alongside the rate benchmarking. If the reset demands rate cuts as a condition, that is a founder decision, escalated, not conceded in a draft.
   Wrong move: Ignoring the reset because "our relationship is with marketing", or badmouthing the process to the champion.

3. **Results cannot be measured.**
   Right move: Build the recap on operational evidence: dates hit, error rates, range coherence, internal adoption, speed of subsequent briefs. State plainly that sales attribution is not claimed. Offer the retainer on capacity and coherence, which never needed the number.
   Wrong move: Reaching for a benchmark uplift or a "typical" figure to fill the gap. One invented number converts the whole recap into fiction.

4. **Client insources mid-retainer.**
   Right move: Congratulate, cooperate, and reshape. Offer the guardianship layer an in-house team still needs (senior review, system memory, peak overflow), hand over cleanly what the terms require, and keep the exit professional. Insourced clients return at peaks and after the in-house hire leaves.
   Wrong move: Defending territory, slowing handover, or treating the in-house hire as a rival to undermine.

5. **Retainer usage sits at 30 percent.**
   Right move: Raise it yourself, at once, with the usage numbers and a resizing proposal: smaller block, different role mix, or pause to a dated restart. The playbook's 70 percent, two-month trigger exists for this; at 30 percent do not wait for the second month.
   Wrong move: Enjoying the quiet margin and hoping nobody audits. Somebody always audits, and the studio becomes the story they tell about retainers.

6. **Retainer usage at 180 percent, team drowning.**
   Right move: Use the overage mechanism transparently: report usage weekly for a month, quote the excess at standard rates with approval first, and propose a block resize at the review with the data in front of both parties.
   Wrong move: Absorbing the overflow silently "for the relationship", then presenting a resentful studio and a surprise invoice later.

7. **The client asks for a discount "because it is regular".**
   Right move: Answer with structure, not rate: reserved capacity, the team that knows the range, one invoice, priority at gates. If budget is the real issue, shrink the block or drop a route from scope. The rate sheet does not move (Scope and Pricing Architect, standing rule 3).
   Wrong move: A "retainer rate" that is the day rate minus a percentage. It re-prices every future conversation and cannot be un-given.

8. **Results were strong but belong to a client's confidential launch.**
   Right move: The recap for external use runs anonymised or omits the claims, exactly at the permission level granted. Inside the pitch with that client, their own results are fully usable; they simply never travel.
   Wrong move: Treating a warm verbal "sure, use it" about the project as permission to publish a sales figure in a deck.

9. **The project went badly and the user wants to pitch anyway.**
   Right move: Refuse the pitch shape, build the repair plan instead (Tree 1). Offer this skill's help with the repair conversation and a dated return. Explain the cost plainly: a pitch now spends the relationship to save a quarter's revenue.
   Wrong move: A recap that quietly edits out the failure the client lived. They notice, and the true claims die with the false framing.

10. **Two clients in the same category both want guardianship.**
    Right move: Check the conflict position before designing either retainer: existing exclusivity promises, category conflicts on the fact sheet, what each client knows. Surface it to the user as a decision with the facts, before any proposal is drafted.
    Wrong move: Signing both and planning to keep the files separate and the conscience clear.

11. **The champion asks you to inflate the recap for their internal meeting.**
    Right move: Decline the inflation, offer the strongest true version: better evidence selection, their wins foregrounded, operational facts added. Explain that an inflated number discovered by finance kills the proposal and their credibility with it.
    Wrong move: "Helping" with a rounded-up figure or a quote they did not quite say. You have made the champion a liar in their own building.

12. **Finance demands proof the retainer is cheaper than ad hoc.**
    Right move: Build the comparison from the client's own project record: what ad hoc cost in fees, rush charges, and re-briefing time across the last cycle, against the block price. If ad hoc genuinely was cheaper for their volume, say so and shrink the block or walk away from the shape.
    Wrong move: A generic "retainers save 20 percent" claim. Unfalsifiable, unsourced, and exactly what finance is paid to reject.

13. **The retainer works but the client wants native source files added free.**
    Right move: Source files follow the rate sheet policy: excluded, or priced per design family, via the Scope and Pricing Architect structures. If files transfer, the guardianship scope needs re-stating, because the client can now alter the system without the check.
    Wrong move: Bundling the files in "as a goodwill gesture" and keeping a guardianship promise the studio can no longer keep.

14. **A seasonal client wants the block only in peak months.**
    Right move: Design the rhythm honestly: guardianship base year-round at the smallest size, plus pre-agreed peak blocks in named months, priced at standard rates, diarised against their calendar. That is a structure, not a discount.
    Wrong move: Selling a full-year block sized for peak and letting the quiet months sit at 20 percent usage until the client cancels in anger.

15. **The decision is stuck at "maybe" for two cycles.**
    Right move: Name the missing input directly with the champion: budget route, authority, timing, or doubt about need. Supply what is yours to supply, agree a decision date, and after that date treat it as a no with a door-open plan.
    Wrong move: Monthly "just checking in" messages that train the client to ignore the studio's name.

16. **The client counters with a much smaller block than the work needs.**
    Right move: Accept a small starter if it covers one evidenced route properly (Tree 2, PITCH SMALLER logic). If it covers everything thinly, re-scope the routes it includes instead of spreading days across all of them.
    Wrong move: Taking the small fee for the full promise. The first missed deadline inside an under-sized block is billed to the studio's reputation, not to the client's arithmetic.

17. **Results evidence lives in the client's systems you cannot access.**
    Right move: Ask the champion for the specific exports or screenshots, list exactly what is needed and why, and build the recap from what arrives. Mode is Manual for that material; declare it. Work with dated exports rather than asking for credentials, which you never do.
    Wrong move: Estimating the numbers "from the launch's scale" or asking for a login to go and look.

18. **The project sponsor was the founder, who now wants the retainer to cover their other company too.**
    Right move: Two organisations means two scopes, or one scope that names both entities and the covered brands explicitly, priced from the combined map. Get the covered list in writing before work crosses over.
    Wrong move: A friendly "of course, send it over" that turns one retainer into an unscoped group arrangement.

19. **Usage is healthy but the work has drifted off-brief into general design support.**
    Right move: Name the drift at the monthly review with examples, and either re-scope the retainer to the work that is actually valued (a legitimate redesign of the block) or re-apply the in and out lists kindly and consistently.
    Wrong move: Letting the block become an unpriced general-purpose design department, then resenting the client for using what the studio silently provided.

20. **The user wants a guaranteed outcome added to the proposal ("this retainer will grow your sales").**
    Right move: Refuse the guarantee, and write the strongest honest alternative: what the retainer buys (capacity, coherence, speed, a team that knows the range) and the evidence from the finished project. Guarantees the studio cannot control are a debt collected later, in trust.
    Wrong move: Softening it to "expected to drive growth" and calling that honest. It is the same promise wearing a cardigan.
