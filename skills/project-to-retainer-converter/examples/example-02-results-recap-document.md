# Example 2: The Results Recap Document Itself

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative. Prices are calculated from the fictional rate sheet shown in this example, not offered as market rates.

## The studio (fictional rate sheet)
Hartwell Studio. Currency USD, prices exclude tax.
- Creative director 950/day, strategist 800/day, senior designer 750/day, designer 550/day, artworker 450/day, project manager 500/day
- Minimum booking unit: half day. Rates are never discounted.
- Retainer terms for this example: monthly fee invoiced in advance, unused days do not roll over, quarterly review with a 70 percent usage trigger.

## Messy input (email from the project lead to the founder)
"Build the actual recap document for Bellweather Pantry, the document we send. Facts: the 24 SKU sauce and marinade relaunch hit the retailer reset date, artwork went to print with zero errors at proof or on shelf, and I have before and after shelf photos from the client's field team. Their brand manager, Tom Okafor, gave a quote and said by email we can use it with his name and title. One problem: in a call he said sales are 'up double digits, easily', but he never sent a figure, a period, or permission. I left it out, but the team keep asking why."

## Reasoning
1. A recap is an evidence document, not a brochure. Every claim below carries a footnote naming its evidence source and permission status, so the client can forward it internally without anyone taking the studio's word for anything.
2. The shelf photos do the visual work. The document describes what they show, in plain language, and credits the source. It does not invent a narrative around them.
3. The artwork error claim is countable: 24 SKUs released, zero errors recorded at proof or after print. That is the kind of claim a pitch can build on, because guardianship is, at its core, error prevention and consistency.
4. The verbal sales comment is excluded, deliberately. It has no precise figure, no period, no source document, and no permission. The critique below explains why.
5. The document ends with one bridge line into a retainer conversation, and nothing more. It does not smuggle a fee or terms into a document the client may circulate. Pricing belongs in the separate scope that follows.

## The maths (internal, shown here for training)
The countable claims in the recap:
- SKUs released: 24
- Artwork errors recorded at proof or on shelf: 0
- Error rate: 0 divided by 24 = 0 percent
- Reset date: hit, with all 24 SKUs supplied print ready before the retailer's file deadline (project tracker, delivery log)

No other numbers are stated in the recap, because no other numbers passed the evidence and permission test above.

## Output: the recap document (as sent, excerpted only for the footnotes layout)

**Bellweather Pantry relaunch: results recap, Hartwell Studio**

**The range, before and after.** The before photographs show the previous range in fixture: three label colours competing across the shelf block, variant names set at two different sizes, and the brand mark appearing in two positions. The after photographs, taken in the same store format after the reset, show the redesigned range as one shelf block: a single label system across all 24 SKUs, variant names at a consistent size and position, and the brand mark in one position throughout. [1]

**Delivered to the reset.** All 24 SKUs were supplied print ready ahead of the retailer's file deadline, and the full redesigned range was in fixture for the planned reset date. [2]

**Artwork accuracy.** Across the 24 SKUs released, zero artwork errors were recorded at proof stage or found on shelf after print. [3]

**What the client said.** "Hartwell treated the reset date as fixed and the details as their problem, not ours. The range finally looks like one range, and our retailers noticed before our customers did." Tom Okafor, Brand Manager, Bellweather Pantry. [4]

**Bridge line.** The range now has a system worth protecting: the same consistency these photographs show is what ongoing guardianship maintains as new SKUs, variants, and retailer versions are added. We would welcome a short conversation about what looking after the range would involve.

**Footnotes, evidence and permission**
[1] Photographs supplied by the client's field team, before set taken at project start, after set taken in the week of the reset. Permission: client supplied the images for use in this recap and in the follow up conversation.
[2] Source: studio project tracker and printer delivery log, file supply dates recorded per SKU. Permission: delivery facts from the studio's own project record, no client confidential data used.
[3] Source: studio artwork release log and the client's post print shelf check, which reported no errors. Permission: figure confirmed in writing by the client for use in this recap. Inside this fictional example, this result is part of the fiction.
[4] Quote source: email from the named client contact. Permission: written permission to use the quote with name and title given.

## Critique
What was deliberately left out, and why: the verbal comment that sales were "up double digits, easily" appears nowhere. It fails every test the included claims pass: no exact figure, no period, no baseline, no source document, and no permission. Printing it would have made the recap stronger for one meeting and weaker forever after, because the first reader who asked "where is that from?" would have undermined every properly sourced claim on the page.
Strengths: each claim is narrow, footnoted, and checkable, and the bridge line follows from the strongest operational claim, the zero error record, rather than from hype. The client can forward the document without edits, which is what a recap is for.
Watch outs: permission is specific, not general. Permission for this recap does not cover a public case study, a website, or social posts. Those need their own permission, and the studio should record exactly what was granted, where, and by whom.
