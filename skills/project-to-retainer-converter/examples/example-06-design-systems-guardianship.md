# Example 6: Brand and Design Systems Guardianship

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative. Prices are calculated from the fictional rate sheet shown in this example, not offered as market rates.

## The studio (fictional rate sheet)
Hartwell Studio, USD, tax excluded. Standard rates, never discounted: creative director 950/day, strategist 800/day, senior designer 750/day, designer 550/day, artworker 450/day, project manager 500/day. Half day minimum; overage at standard rates, half day minimum, quoted and approved in writing first.

## Messy input (handover note from the project lead)
"Northbeam Financial rebrand is live. New identity, new Figma component library, 140 components, templates for product, marketing, and investor decks. Their head of brand is worried it will drift within a year. Three product teams are already asking for new components, they expand into a second market next year, and every team has its own designer who 'just tweaks things'. She asked: 'Can you look after the system for us, so it stays one system?' She wants the library kept clean and someone to say no, kindly, with reasons."

## Reasoning
1. This is guardianship, not general design supply, and the boundary must stay bright. The retainer stewards the system: component library, templates, usage guidance, review of work made from it. It is not an overflow design team for three product teams, or the block is consumed by feature work in month one.
2. Routing is the operating system here. All requests go into one queue owned by the client's head of brand and the studio project manager. Each is logged, estimated in days, and either drawn from the block, batched to the monthly systems review, or quoted separately. No queue, no work. That rule stops drift, because drift enters as small direct favours.
3. The Figma audit method needs stating before the first review. In connected mode, where the client grants access to the Figma files or an analytics view, the studio audits directly: component usage, detached instances, local styles created outside the library, and template compliance are checked in the files themselves. In manual mode, the audit runs from exports and screenshots the client supplies, plus a walkthrough call. Manual mode covers less, and can miss files the studio never sees. The proposal names which mode applies as [TO CONFIRM], so the client understands what access buys: coverage, not a different rate.
4. A monthly systems review is the decision point: new component requests are approved, adapted from existing components, or declined with reasons; library changes are summarised; drift is listed with file, owner, and fix. The strategist allocation exists for second market work, where system questions become positioning questions, not design tweaks.
5. Rates, review, and exit follow the standard model: standard rates unchanged, overage at standard rates, half day minimum, rollover marked [TO CONFIRM], quarterly review with usage trigger, exit at a quarter end on written notice, with a handover note covering file locations and pending queue items.

## The maths
Monthly block:
- Creative director: 0.5 x 950 = 475
- Strategist: 1 x 800 = 800
- Senior designer: 2 x 750 = 1,500
- Designer: 2 x 550 = 1,100
- Artworker: 1 x 450 = 450
- Project manager: 1 x 500 = 500
- Monthly total: 7.5 days, 4,825
- Quarterly total: 4,825 x 3 = 14,475
Overage illustration: one extra senior designer day for a genuinely new component family, 1 x 750 = 750.

## Output excerpt (proposal)
**Design systems guardianship for Northbeam Financial: 4,825 per month**, invoiced monthly in advance. A reserved block of 7.5 days per month (creative director 0.5, strategist 1, senior designer 2, designer 2, artworker 1, project manager 1), booked against the Northbeam brand and Figma library.
**In scope, drawn from the block:** stewardship of the Figma component library (naming, organisation, version notes, deprecation); assessment of new component requests through the shared queue, with build of approved components up to [TO CONFIRM: number] per month; template maintenance; a monthly Figma audit, in [connected / manual] mode [TO CONFIRM], reporting detached components, off library styles, and drift; one monthly systems review call with a written decisions log.
**Out of scope, quoted separately:** product feature design and UX work; new identities or sub brands; a library rebuild or platform migration; training beyond the monthly review; markets or units not named in the contractual scope.
**How the block works:** every request enters the queue through your head of brand to our project manager, who confirms estimated days before work starts. A usage note accompanies each invoice. Overage is at standard rates, half day minimum, quoted and approved in writing first. Rollover: [TO CONFIRM].
**Quarterly review and exit:** each quarter we review usage, library health, queue volume, and coming plans, and either side may propose resizing the block. If usage runs under 70 percent of the block for two consecutive months, we will raise resizing at the review ourselves. Either party may end the retainer at the end of a quarter by giving written notice as set out in the contractual scope (the retainer scope from the Scope and Pricing Architect module). Drafting aid, have counsel review before use.

## Critique
Strengths: the retainer sells a protected asset, not hours. The pitch frames the block as keeping a full project fee's worth of system as one system, with a queue, an audit, and a decisions log as proof each month.
Watch outs: the queue will be tested socially before it is tested on volume. A product lead will message a designer directly, and if that favour is done once, the queue is decorative. The project manager must redirect kindly and visibly in month one, with the head of brand backing the rule. Second: component requests can hide product strategy; a new dashboard pattern may really be a new product tier, which is why the monthly review, not the queue alone, decides what gets built.
