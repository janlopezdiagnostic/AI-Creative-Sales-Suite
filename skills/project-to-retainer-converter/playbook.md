# Playbook: Project to Retainer Converter

This playbook is the method behind the skill. It is written for packaging and brand design studios; fashion and agency differences are flagged where they bite. The principle throughout: a retainer is not sold, it is recognised. The client already has the ongoing work. Your job is to make the work visible, cost the current way of buying it, and offer a calmer structure at the same rates.

## Part 1. The results recap method

The recap is the foundation. Everything after it (the map, the proposal, the meeting) borrows its credibility, so it is built like evidence, not like marketing.

### What counts as evidence

Evidence, in descending order of strength:

1. **Dated operational facts.** What shipped, in what quantity, by what date. Twelve SKUs on shelf for the spring reset, artwork locked 19 September, zero artwork errors across the range, files accepted by the printer first pass. These are checkable and the client knows they are true because they lived them.
2. **Client-side outcomes the client can verify.** Distribution gained or held, listings won, sell-through the client shared with you, internal adoption (other teams or markets requesting the system), regulatory approvals passed. Cite only with permission, at the permission level given.
3. **Client testimony.** A quote, an email line, a comment in a review meeting, recorded with who said it and when. Never paraphrase a compliment into a stronger claim. "The launch went smoothly" does not become "our smoothest launch ever" in your recap.
4. **Studio-side observations.** What the studio noticed: the range held together under late copy changes, the guidelines absorbed three new SKUs without a redesign. Label these as the studio's view. They support judgement, they are not results.

What does not count: industry benchmarks presented as the client's results, uplifts "typically seen", anything the client said in a corridor that they would not recognise in writing, and anything from another client's project.

### Before and after

Before/after is the strongest recap device in visual work and the easiest to misuse. Rules:

- Show the shelf, the range, or the system as it was, and as it is, with dates. Photography the client has already approved for circulation, or renders from the delivered files.
- Describe what changed in craft terms a brand manager can repeat internally: hierarchy clarified, range architecture made extendable, colour blocking at shelf distance.
- Never mock the before. The before was usually the client's own earlier work, sometimes their own design. "From fragmented to coherent" is a description. "From amateur to professional" is an insult wearing a case study's clothes.

### Outcomes that are not numbers

Many packaging and brand projects cannot be tied to a clean sales figure, because distribution, pricing, and media all move at once. Do not force a number. The honest outcome set is usually: the launch happened on the immovable date, the range can now grow without breaking, the internal team can brief faster because the system exists, and the error and rework rate fell. Clients trust a recap that names what the work can and cannot claim. Procurement, later, trusts it too.

### Getting permission to cite

Ask once, specifically, at the warm moment (usually the sign-off or launch review), and record the answer next to each claim:

- "May we cite the two additional retailer listings in our own materials?" is a different question from "may we cite sales."
- Named, anonymised ("a national grocery brand"), or internal only. Three different permissions. Do not upgrade one into another.
- A client quote needs the quoter's agreement to the exact words, not a vibe.
- If permission is refused, the claim still works inside the pitch meeting with that client, because they know it is theirs. It simply never travels. Mark it internal only and move on.

### The repair paragraph

If the project had failures, the recap contains a short paragraph, before the results, that names them in plain terms, states what the studio did about them, and states what changed in the studio's process. No adjectives, no excuses, no blaming the client's copy or the printer unless the record genuinely shows it and it matters. This paragraph is not a confession ritual. It is proof the studio tracks itself, which is precisely what a guardianship client is buying. Skipping it when the client lived the failure destroys the recap's credibility entirely; they stop believing the true parts too.

## Part 2. Expansion mapping

The expansion map answers one question: where is this client's recurring creative work, really? Build it from the client's world, not from the studio's service list.

### The five routes

1. **More of the same range.** Line extensions, flavour and format variants, size changes, promotional and seasonal editions of what you just built. In packaging this is the most reliable route: a living range generates artwork the way a garden generates weeds. Evidence: the client's launch cadence during the project, their NPD talk, the retailer's category calendar.
2. **Sister brands and adjacent ranges.** The client organisation owns other brands that share the buyer, the design leadership, or the production base. Evidence: the group's portfolio, who attended your project's meetings from outside the brand team, internal comments about "doing the same for X". This route needs a new internal conversation; map the owner, do not assume the budget travels.
3. **Other markets.** Rollout to new countries or channels: bilingual or market-specific panels, retailer-exclusive versions, e-commerce imagery sets. Evidence: distribution announcements, market hires, the client's own launch language. Market work is unglamorous, deadline-driven, and perfectly suited to a block of days.
4. **Seasonal programmes.** Holiday editions, summer variants, back-to-school, retailer events. Seasonal work is dated a year ahead, which makes it the best sizing evidence a retainer can have. If you can name four dated seasonal moments in the next twelve months, the retainer nearly sizes itself.
5. **Adjacent services.** The work around the work: guidelines guardianship (checking anything anyone produces against the system), NPD pipeline support (early-stage design input before projects exist), artwork operations (the standing production layer: updates, regulatory panels, file management), design systems stewardship for brand and product teams. Adjacent services convert because the client felt their absence during the project, usually at 11pm before a deadline.

### Mapping discipline

Every route gets four fields: evidence, owner, trigger, size. Evidence it exists (a fact, not a hope). The likely owner on the client side, by role and name if sourced. The timing trigger that starts the work. A size estimate in days per month, derived from the route's actual cadence, for example two SKU adaptations a quarter is a different size from two a month. Routes that cannot fill all four fields go on the map marked speculation, visibly, and are excluded from the retainer sizing. A map with two evidenced routes and one speculation beats a map with seven wishes, every time, because the champion has to defend it internally.

## Part 3. Retainer structures and their economics

Four structures cover nearly every case. All are priced from the rate sheet at standard rates. What changes is the shape of the commitment, never the rate.

### Guardianship

A modest monthly block weighted to senior review time plus artwork support, sold on coherence: the range or brand system stays intact as many hands touch it. In scope: checks on new and third-party artwork against the guidelines, drift flagged in writing, small updates, a periodic range or system review. Economics: small block, high seniority mix, very sticky, because the studio becomes the memory of the system. Risk: "guardianship" expanding by politeness into unpaid design. The in and out lists carry the protection; the word alone does not.

### Pipeline

A block sized to a forecast flow of projects: NPD stages, line extensions, campaign refreshes moving through predictable gates. Weighted to designer and strategist time. Economics: larger block, better utilisation for the studio, and the client gets priority at gates instead of re-briefing from zero each time. Risk: the pipeline stalls and the client resents the fee. The quarterly review with the usage trigger is the release valve, and it must be real.

### Embedded days

Named people, named days per week or month, working inside the client's rhythm: their standups, their tools, their queue. Suits design systems and artwork operations. Economics: the most predictable revenue and the most dangerous dependency; one client can quietly become the studio's schedule. Cap embedded share of studio capacity deliberately, and keep the exit terms clean.

### Hybrid

The usual winner for packaging: a small guardianship base (senior check time, artwork ops) plus a flex layer drawn as approved overage at standard rates for launch peaks. Economics: the base keeps the relationship warm and the system coherent; the flex absorbs seasonality without forcing the client to buy peak capacity all year. Risk: the flex becoming the real retainer while the base sits unused, which the usage note exposes within two months.

### Sizing honestly

Size from the map, not from the revenue target. Take the evidenced routes, convert cadence to days per month by role, then stress the result against the project you just ran: if the project consumed about nine studio days a month at its working pace and the ongoing cadence is visibly lower, a block around half to two-thirds of that pace is usually the honest start, with the quarterly review there to grow it. A retainer that starts slightly small and grows at the first review renews. A retainer that starts fat and sits at 40 percent usage dies at the first budget round, and takes the relationship with it.

### A worked sizing walkthrough

Take a packaging client whose project just shipped: a redesign that consumed, at working pace, roughly 40 studio days over four months, weighted to senior designer and artworker time. The expansion map shows: two SKU adaptations a quarter (each about a day and a half of designer plus artworker time, so about one day a month), seasonal sleeves twice a year (about four days each, so under a day a month averaged, but arriving as peaks), pack copy and regulatory updates most months (half an artworker day), and a range that three other suppliers now touch (guardianship checks, half a senior designer day a month, plus a monthly review rhythm in project management). The honest base is therefore not the project's ten days a month; it is roughly three to four days a month, mixed as senior review, designer, artworker, and a slice of project management, with the seasonal peaks handled as flex overage agreed in advance. Pitch that, and the quarterly review will likely grow it. Pitch ten days because the project felt big, and the usage note will be arguing against you by month two.

## Part 4. Timing

Timing is half the conversion. The same proposal, three weeks apart, is a different proposal.

- **The default window is two to six weeks after launch.** Results are arriving, the internal credit is being assigned, and the people who shipped are still the people in the client's head. Before launch you are pitching on hope; after three months you are a memory and the moment is owned by whoever is in the room then.
- **Planning cycles beat enthusiasm.** If the client's planning round closes in October, a brilliant August conversation still needs an October paper trail. Anchor the proposal's decision date to their cycle and say so; it signals you understand how their company actually buys.
- **Budget seasons.** New fiscal year budgets are defended in the quarter before they open. A retainer that is in the budget is boring and safe. A retainer that needs new money mid-year is a fight. Ask when the budget is set and time the formal proposal to land before the freeze, using the post-launch window for the recap conversation that earns the right to submit it.
- **Seasonal calendars by vertical.** Packaging: work backwards from retailer resets, commonly spring and autumn, with artwork lock eight to twelve weeks ahead (verify per retailer); a guardianship pitch lands best just after a reset ships, when next reset's workload is visible. Fashion: the retainer rhythm follows the collection calendar, two main seasons plus capsules, pitched after sell-through of the season you worked on is known. Brand and product design: pitch against roadmap planning and new-market entries, which are usually quarterly.
- **When to wait.** Mid-dispute, mid-freeze, mid-restructure, and the week a champion leaves are all wait signals. Waiting, communicated plainly with a date, is a professional act. Pitching through them is not persistence, it is deafness.

## Part 5. The pitch meeting

Forty-five minutes, four movements, one ask.

1. **The recap (ten minutes).** What shipped, what it did, evidence on the table. Let the client correct and add; their additions become your best material and their ownership of the story. Do not present the repair paragraph as an apology performance; state it, state the fix, move on.
2. **The water they are already swimming in (ten minutes).** Walk the expansion map as their workload, not your opportunity: "Between now and the autumn reset you have these SKUs, this seasonal edition, and the market variants. Here is how that work is currently getting done." The cost of ad hoc (re-briefing, queueing behind other clients, inconsistent artwork, rush fees when the reset moves) is stated factually from the project record, never as a threat.
3. **The structure (fifteen minutes).** Present the designed retainer: the block, the role mix, what it covers, what it does not, how overage and the quarterly review work, and the exit. Present the exit early and plainly. A client who hears a clean exit relaxes about entering; a client who cannot find the exit assumes there is a trap.
4. **The ask (ten minutes).** One decision, dated: approve the shape in the room, with the paper to follow from the Scope and Pricing Architect templates, or name the internal step (finance review, procurement onboarding) and the date it completes. If the answer is a soft yes, the champion brief is what you leave behind, and the follow-up date goes in both diaries before anyone stands up.

Never bring: a rate card defence (rates were never the topic), criticism of the client's in-house team or other suppliers (you may be proposing to work alongside both), or a deadline invented by the studio ("we can only hold this team until Friday") unless capacity scarcity is genuinely true and stated as such.

## Part 6. Handling "we will just call you"

This sentence is the most common retainer refusal and usually means one of four things: the structure feels too big, the timing is wrong, the speaker lacks the authority or budget route, or they genuinely believe ad hoc is equivalent. Diagnose before answering, with one question: "What would need to be true for a standing arrangement to make sense?" Then listen.

If you answer, answer with the difference, concretely: ad hoc buys a place in the queue each time, briefed from memory, at whatever the calendar allows, with rush exposure when the retailer's date moves. The retainer buys reserved capacity, a team that carries the range in its head, and one invoice. Both are legitimate choices. Some clients should stay ad hoc, and saying so when the map shows thin ongoing work builds more trust than a conversion would.

Then shrink the commitment, not the rate: a one-quarter starter block at the smallest honest size, the quarterly review as the decision point, exit at the quarter end. A starter that is used renews into the real shape. And if the answer stays no, convert it into a dated arrangement anyway: "Then let us agree I will check in before the spring reset artwork window, and if a big quarter appears, you call me before it lands, not during it." That is a door left open with a date on it, which is most of what a declined retainer should become.

## Part 7. Building the internal champion

The retainer is usually decided in a room you are not in. The champion carries it there, so build the champion deliberately.

- **Know what the champion gains.** Their launches get easier, their range stays coherent, their name attaches to a system that works. The champion brief (template) is written in their currency: their wins restated, the workload made visible, the finance questions pre-answered.
- **Arm, do not script.** Give them the one-pager, the cost-of-ad-hoc comparison built from their own project record, and the answers to the three questions finance always asks (why not per project, what happens if we do not use it, how do we stop). A champion who sounds scripted loses the room.
- **Make them look good upward.** The recap is partly their internal evidence. Deliver it in a format they can forward unedited, with their role accurately credited. Studios that hoard credit get one project; studios that distribute it get the account.
- **Protect the champion from risk.** The starter size, the usage trigger, and the clean exit exist so that championing you is a low-risk act for their career. Say that plainly. And if the champion leaves, the relationship work in the edge cases applies: the retainer must be re-earned with the successor quickly, on the recap's evidence, not on history.

## Part 8. After the decision

Yes: paper it through the Scope and Pricing Architect retainer template, confirm the first month's expected requests, and run the first usage note perfectly. Retainers are won at the pitch and lost in month two's admin.

No: record the reason verbatim, the dated re-entry trigger, and the keep-warm plan in the CRM (with confirmation, per tools.md). Then keep the promise exactly: the check-in happens on the date, with something useful attached (a relevant reset date, a regulatory change, their seasonal moment), not a "just circling back."

Maybe, which is the commonest answer: a maybe is a yes missing one input. Find out which input, supply it if it is yours to supply, and date the next conversation before the meeting ends. A maybe left undated is a no that has not told you yet.

## Part 9. The first ninety days (keeping what you converted)

More retainers are lost in the first quarter than at the pitch, and usually to administration, not to rivals. Four disciplines protect the conversion:

1. **Route everything through the named channel from day one.** The first time a client direct-messages a designer and it works, the usage log, the block, and eventually the retainer's credibility all start leaking. Redirect kindly, every time, and make the official route faster than the unofficial one.
2. **Send the usage note before anyone asks.** Days used, by role, what they produced, block remaining. A client who watches the block being managed trusts the studio with a bigger one. A client who discovers usage at the invoice audits everything afterwards.
3. **Deliver one visible guardianship catch early.** The drift caught, the inconsistent panel spotted before print, the deadline the client had forgotten that the calendar caught. One concrete save in the first quarter is worth more than the entire pitch deck, because it converts the retainer from a cost into a story the champion retells.
4. **Run the first quarterly review like a pitch, not a formality.** Usage, outcomes, next quarter's forecast from the expansion map refreshed, and the resize question asked in both directions. The review is where the retainer becomes a planning instrument instead of a standing charge, and where expansion routes (sister brands, new markets) get their dated moment rather than competing with the renewal for attention.
