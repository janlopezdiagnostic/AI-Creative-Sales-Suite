# Example 3: The Rush Job

All brands, people, and prices in this example are fictional and illustrative. Prices are calculated from the fictional rate sheet shown in this example, not offered as market rates.

## The studio (fictional rate sheet excerpt)
Hartwell Studio. Currency USD, prices exclude tax.
- Creative director 950/day, senior designer 750/day, designer 550/day, artworker 450/day, project manager 500/day
- Included revision rounds: 2 per phase. Deposit 50 percent, taken before work starts, no exceptions on rush work
- Rush: any timeline under 15 working days for a multi SKU job. Multiplier 1.5x on the design phases affected. Source files excluded

## Messy input (phone call summary from the account exec)
"Coldpress Co, cold pressed juice shots. Their previous designer has gone quiet two weeks before a trade show. They have brand assets, a dieline from the bottle supplier, and copy signed off. Six SKUs, two design families. Show opens in 10 working days, printer needs files by day 8. They are stressed and said 'whatever it takes'. Current studio workload: two projects would need to move."

## Reasoning
1. "Whatever it takes" is not a price. The job gets a real base price first, then a visible rush line, so the client chooses with numbers (playbook part 5).
2. Scope is countable: 6 SKUs, 2 families, assets and copy exist, dieline supplied. Fixed fee.
3. Rush applies to the design phases (masters and adaptations), which must be compressed and rescheduled around. Project management and proofing are charged at standard rates but the whole team is scheduled as a block.
4. Deposit rule matters more, not less, under rush: 50 percent before work starts. A client who cannot pay a deposit quickly cannot be prioritised over clients who did.
5. Revision rounds drop to 1 consolidated round per phase in the rush scope, stated openly. Ten days cannot carry two leisurely rounds; the client trades rounds for speed, knowingly.

## The maths (internal, shown here for training)
- Master design, 2 families: per family senior designer 2 days (1,500) + creative director 0.5 day (475) = 1,975; x 2 = 3,950
- Adaptations, 4 further SKUs: per SKU designer 0.5 day (275) + artworker 0.25 day (112.50) = 387.50; x 4 = 1,550
- Design phases subtotal: 5,500
- Rush uplift at 1.5x on design phases: +2,750
- Print proofing: artworker 1 day = 450
- Project management: 1 day = 500
- Total: 9,200. Standard timeline price for the same scope: 6,450.

## Output: scope (client facing excerpt)
**Rush scope, 9,200.** Master design for 2 families, artwork for 6 SKUs, print ready files to your supplier's dieline by day 8, one consolidated revision round per phase, daily progress note.
Price build up, shown openly: design work 5,500; rush uplift 2,750 (our standard 1.5x for timelines under 15 working days; it covers rescheduling two booked projects and evening work); proofing and project management 950.
**The alternative, stated in the same email:** the same scope on our standard 25 working day timeline is 6,450 with 2 revision rounds per phase. If the show deadline moves, tell us and the rush line comes off.
Terms: 50 percent deposit today to start, balance before file release. If feedback is not consolidated or arrives later than the daily slots agreed at kickoff, the day 8 date moves with it; we will tell you the same day.
Not included: print costs, photography, font and image licences; full exclusions per the SOW. Kill fee per the SOW stages applies if the show is cancelled.

## SOW excerpt
"3. Timeline: files to printer by working day 8 from deposit receipt, conditional on client feedback within the daily windows in the kickoff note. 4. Fees: 9,200 comprising design 5,500, rush uplift 2,750, production and management 950."

## Critique
Strengths: the rush fee is a line, not a pad, and it already produced its best outcome in the reasoning: if the client balks, they reveal whether day 8 is real. Rounds were traded openly rather than silently squeezed.
Watch outs: rush estimates hide fatigue errors; the proofing day is doing heavy lifting and should not be the first thing cut in negotiation. Also note what the scope does not promise: it does not promise the printer's turnaround or the show logistics, which are outside the studio's control and correctly absent from the deliverables.
