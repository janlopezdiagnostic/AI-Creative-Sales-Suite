# Edge Cases

Format: trigger, the right move, the wrong move. Rates and terms always come from the studio rate sheet, never from this file.

**1. Net-90 payment demand**
Trigger: Client or procurement states 90 day payment terms as non-negotiable.
Right move: Treat it as a scoping input. Accept only with a purchase order before start, milestones structured to keep exposure small, and the founder aware of the cash cost. Offer 30 day terms with the standard schedule as the alternative.
Wrong move: Sign 30 day terms knowing they pay at 90, or start work with no PO and hope.

**2. "Minor revisions" creep**
Trigger: A stream of small change requests framed as minor revisions after approval.
Right move: Log each one. After the first, reply with the round definition from the SOW and a consolidated change order for the accumulated list.
Wrong move: Absorbing them silently until the project is unprofitable, then raising it all at once in frustration.

**3. IP buyout pressure at handover**
Trigger: Source files demanded at delivery, never agreed, framed as obviously included.
Right move: Point to the SOW files clause, offer the priced buyout from the rate sheet basis, stay warm. See decision tree 5.
Wrong move: Handing files over to end an awkward conversation, or refusing with no priced route offered.

**4. Procurement day-rate benchmarking**
Trigger: Procurement compares the studio's day rate with a freelancer marketplace rate.
Right move: Redirect to deliverables, outcomes, and what their benchmark excludes (strategy, production experience, accountability, revision structure). Offer scope trades, not rate movement.
Wrong move: Defending the rate line by line or discounting to close the spreadsheet gap.

**5. Unpaid pitch invitation**
Trigger: A polished RFP asking several studios for free creative.
Right move: Run decision tree 2. Offer paid discovery. If pitching, scope and protect the pitch itself.
Wrong move: Producing full routes for free because the brand is famous. A famous name does not pay the invoice.

**6. Equity or exposure offer**
Trigger: A startup offers equity, or a brand offers exposure, instead of fees.
Right move: Price the work normally. If the founder wants the bet, it is a separate investment decision with its own paperwork (counsel review), never a substitute written into the SOW fee line.
Wrong move: Discounting against vague equity or exposure with nothing documented.

**7. The budget revealed after the proposal**
Trigger: The client responds to a full price with "our budget is half that".
Right move: Thank them for the number, build the scope that fits it as the Good tier, keep the full tiers visible, and let them choose.
Wrong move: Cutting the fee on the same scope, or asking "what can you do?" and negotiating against yourself.

**8. SKU count growth**
Trigger: The range grows from 12 SKUs to 18 during artwork.
Right move: Change order per additional SKU at the adaptation rate in the original scope. The scope named the count, so this is arithmetic, not argument.
Wrong move: Absorbing the growth because the relationship is new.

**9. The committee with no decision maker**
Trigger: Feedback arrives from five people, contradictory, across separate rounds.
Right move: Invoke the SOW round definition, ask for one named consolidator, and pause artwork until consolidated feedback exists. Offer a reconciliation round as a change order if they cannot consolidate.
Wrong move: Designing to each comment in turn and letting the rounds burn silently.

**10. Rush demand without rush budget**
Trigger: A deadline moves a month earlier; the client expects the same fee.
Right move: Quote the rush line visibly from the rate sheet multiplier, alongside the option of the original date at the original fee. Let them choose with real numbers.
Wrong move: Absorbing the overtime quietly and resenting the client for a choice they were never offered.

**11. Kill after concept approval**
Trigger: The client's budget is cut after they approved a concept.
Right move: Apply the kill fee stage from the SOW, invoice work completed plus the stage percentage, hand over what has been paid for, wish them well.
Wrong move: Waiving the kill fee to "keep the relationship", then discovering the relationship was the budget.

**12. Client supplies broken assets**
Trigger: Client supplied dielines, copy, or data turn out to be wrong, forcing rework.
Right move: Notify immediately in writing, quantify the rework, change order for the fix. The SOW assumptions stated client supplied assets would be correct and final.
Wrong move: Silently fixing supplier errors at studio cost because raising it feels petty mid-project.

**13. Regulatory content changes late**
Trigger: Legal or regulatory review rewrites pack copy after artwork approval.
Right move: Content accuracy is the client's responsibility per the scope; re-artwork from changed content is a change order, quoted with its schedule effect on the print date.
Wrong move: Promising the print date holds while re-artworking a full range for free.

**14. "Our last studio included everything"**
Trigger: The client claims a previous supplier included source files, unlimited rounds, and production support at a lower fee.
Right move: Acknowledge it, walk your own deliverables list, and offer the tier that matches their actual need. Do not litigate a stranger's business model.
Wrong move: Matching an unverifiable bundle by silently stripping your own protections.

**15. Multi-market rollout surprise**
Trigger: Packaging designed for one market is now needed in four, with local claims, languages, and regulations.
Right move: Scope the rollout as its own phase: per market adaptation, translation handling (client supplies or approves translations), local regulatory content owned by the client.
Wrong move: Treating extra markets as adaptations at the single market price.

**16. The champion leaves**
Trigger: Your client contact leaves mid-project; the successor questions the scope and price.
Right move: Send the successor the SOW and a short status summary (done, approved, remaining). The paperwork is the continuity.
Wrong move: Re-selling the whole project verbally and accepting the successor's memory of the deal.

**17. Deposit resistance**
Trigger: The client wants work to start before the deposit clears, citing internal process.
Right move: Offer what can legitimately start: a kickoff meeting booked, discovery reading, a start date held for a stated number of days. Design work starts on deposit, as scoped.
Wrong move: Starting design "in good faith" for a client whose first act is not meeting the first term.

**18. Discount for a logo on the studio site**
Trigger: A famous brand offers a low fee, arguing the portfolio value.
Right move: Portfolio value is real but it is not a line item. If the founder wants the logo, buy it consciously with a scope trade (smaller scope at the lower fee), never a rate cut, and get case study permission in writing.
Wrong move: Full scope at a fame discount with no case study rights secured.

**19. Competitor undercut claim**
Trigger: "Another studio quoted 40 percent less."
Right move: Ask what their quote includes, walk your deliverables list, offer the matching reduced scope at a matching price. Many undercut quotes are smaller scopes presented as if they covered the same work.
Wrong move: Matching the number blind on an unchanged scope.

**20. Free work for a charity arm or founder's side project**
Trigger: A paying client asks for free design for an unrelated side project.
Right move: Decide as a studio policy question (a set number of pro bono projects a year, chosen by the founder), scope it lightly in writing anyway, and keep it out of the commercial project's terms.
Wrong move: Bolting free work onto a paid SOW where it will expand and sour the main relationship.

**21. Verbal approval, missing signature**
Trigger: The client says "approved, go ahead" by phone and the SOW is unsigned.
Right move: Confirm by email immediately ("Per our call, you approved X; work starts; SOW attached for signature"), and treat signature chasing as a weekly admin task until it lands.
Wrong move: Running a full project on a verbal with no written trail.

**22. Payment stops mid-project**
Trigger: A milestone invoice goes overdue while artwork continues.
Right move: Use the pause clause from the SOW at the stated number of days, communicate it once, clearly, with the restart condition (payment received, dates re-forecast). Calm, procedural, no threats.
Wrong move: Continuing to deliver while invoices age, which converts a pause of days into a write-off of weeks.

**23. AI and stock asset licence traps**
Trigger: The design uses AI generated imagery or stock assets whose licences restrict merchandise, resale, or trademark use.
Right move: Clear licence fitness for the actual use before artwork approval, record the licences in the project file, and pass licence purchases through as agreed. Flag licence limits to the client in writing.
Wrong move: Assuming "we bought the stock" covers every use, and discovering the restriction at print.

**24. The never-ending launch support**
Trigger: After final delivery, daily small requests continue: a resize here, a social crop there.
Right move: Close the project formally (final invoice, delivery note, what is included is now complete), then offer the retainer or a day rate support block. Launch goodwill covers the first small ask, logged; the pattern gets a structure.
Wrong move: Letting an undefined tail run for months because closing the project feels abrupt.

**25. Client wants the skill's own rate logic exposed**
Trigger: A sophisticated client asks to see the day maths behind a fixed fee.
Right move: The studio chooses its transparency model in advance and stays consistent: either share phase level estimates openly, or present the fee as a deliverables price and walk the deliverables. Either is defensible; improvising under pressure is not.
Wrong move: Inventing a breakdown on the spot that contradicts the fee, or sharing another client's pricing as a benchmark. Other clients' numbers are never disclosed, in any form.
