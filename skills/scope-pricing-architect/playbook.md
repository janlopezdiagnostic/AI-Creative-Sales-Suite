# The Scope and Pricing Playbook

This is the method behind the skill. Read it once end to end. After that, the decision trees and templates carry most of the day to day weight.

The core belief: studios rarely lose money on the work they priced. They lose money on the work they gave away without noticing: the extra SKU, the fourth revision round, the source files handed over free, the "quick favour" in the launch week. Scoping is the discipline of noticing in advance, on paper, while there is still time to price it or decline it politely.

## Part 1. Deliverables decomposition

Never scope a project as a single lump ("packaging design, one fee"). Decompose it into deliverables a stranger could count. For packaging work, the standard families are:

### Strategy and foundations
- Category and competitor audit (shelf, online, or both)
- Positioning and messaging hierarchy: what the pack must say, in what order
- Architecture decisions: how the range is organised, what differentiates SKUs visually
Strategy is frequently the most under-scoped phase because clients treat it as "the thinking before the real work". Price it as its own phase with its own approval gate. Work that is not approved at strategy stage gets relitigated at artwork stage, at artwork prices, with strategy effort.

### Naming
- Name generation (state how many candidates, how many rounds)
- Shortlist screening support (the studio can support trademark screening, it cannot give legal clearance; say so)
- Naming architecture for future extensions
Naming looks small and expands fast. Cap the candidate count and the rounds in writing.

### Structural design
- Form, format, and pack structure exploration
- Liaison with structural engineers, carton or label suppliers, and co-packers
- Mockups and physical dummies (state how many, and who pays for materials)
Structural work involves third parties and physical iterations. Scope the number of structural routes and the number of physical iterations, and pass material costs through as out of pocket expenses with an approval threshold.

### Artwork and design per SKU
This is where packaging scopes live or die. Artwork is priced per SKU or per design family plus adaptations, never as "the range" without a count.
- Master design per design family
- Adaptation per additional SKU (state what varies: flavour, size, colourway, claims)
- Front of pack, back of pack, and each panel or face treated as part of that SKU's artwork, but information changes late in the process are revisions or changes, not free updates
Write the SKU count into the scope as a number. If the count grows, the change order writes itself, because the original scope said 12 and the client is now asking for 14.

### Dielines and technical setup
- Working to supplier dielines, or creating dielines if structural is in scope
- Barcode, regulatory panels, nutrition or ingredient layouts as placed content (the client owns the accuracy of regulated content; the studio owns placing it correctly)
- Print ready file preparation per SKU
State whose dieline is authoritative and what happens if the supplier changes it late. A late dieline change that forces re-artwork across a range is a change order, and the scope should say so before it happens.

### Print and production support
- Colour proofing rounds (state how many)
- Press passes or remote approvals (state whether included, and travel treatment)
- Printer liaison time, estimated honestly; production support is real work and a common giveaway
Offer production support as an explicit line or a priced option. Studios that bury it give it away; studios that list it get paid for it and look more professional, not less.

### Guidelines and rollout
- Brand or packaging guidelines document (state length or scope: core rules versus full system)
- Template files for future SKUs
- Handover session or training
Guidelines are the natural Best tier and the natural phase 2. They also reduce future scope creep, because the client can self serve the small jobs.

## Part 2. Pricing structures

### Fixed fee
Best when deliverables are countable and the process is understood. Requirements: a deliverables inventory, capped revision rounds, stated assumptions. The fee is built bottom up (time per phase at the rate sheet rates, plus project management, plus bought in costs) and presented top down (one fee per phase or per tier). Never show the client the internal day maths unless the studio's model is openly time based. Risk: the studio carries overrun risk, which is exactly why the assumptions and exclusions matter.

### Day rate
Best for open ended, exploratory, or embedded work: ongoing brand support, undefined pipelines, "help us figure it out" engagements. Protect it with a minimum booking unit, a weekly or monthly cap the client approves, and a simple reporting habit (days used, work done, every week). Day rate work without a cap and reporting drifts into an argument about value; with them, it is the calmest model there is.

### Retainer
Best when volume is real and recurring: monthly SKU adaptations, campaign cycles, content and pack updates. Structure: a defined pool (days or deliverable credits), a defined rate for overage, a rollover rule (no rollover, or one month, stated plainly), and a quarterly review. A retainer is a capacity reservation, not a discount scheme. If the client wants retainer pricing with project behaviour (everything urgent, nothing forecast), the structure is wrong and will be renegotiated in pain within two quarters.

### Paid discovery or paid first phase
When the brief cannot be priced honestly (unknown SKU count, undecided architecture, internal disagreement on the client side), sell a fixed price discovery phase whose output is the thing the full scope needs: audit, architecture, deliverables inventory. Discovery is not a discounted sample of the work. It is a product with its own deliverable: the findings and the scoped plan. Fee for discovery can be credited against the full project if the studio chooses; if offered, state it, because it converts well and costs little.

### Blended structures
Common and legitimate: fixed fee for design phases, day rate for production support, retainer offered at the end for rollout. Say which model governs which phase in the scope. Ambiguity about the model is where disputes breed.

## Part 3. Revision round economics

A revision round is one consolidated set of feedback from one named client decision maker, responded to once. Everything else is either a new round or a change.

Why this matters: three stakeholders sending separate comments on separate days can triple design time while the client believes they used "one round, roughly". The scope must define a round, cap the included rounds per phase, and price additional rounds. Pricing extra rounds is not greed; it is the only language that makes consolidated feedback happen.

Set included rounds by phase risk. Concept phases tolerate more exploration; artwork phases should be tighter, because late artwork changes cascade into dielines, proofs, and print dates. If the client wants unlimited revisions, the honest responses are: a day rate model, or a fixed fee with a large, explicit contingency they can see. Unlimited revisions inside a lean fixed fee is how studios work free weekends in month three.

Internal rule for estimators: if a client has a committee, assume feedback will be contradictory at least once and price the reconciliation round into Better and Best tiers, where the studio can afford to be gracious.

## Part 4. Usage and IP licensing

Drafting aid throughout this part; have counsel review the studio's standard terms.

### What the client is actually buying
By default, the client buys the final approved artwork, for the agreed use: the products, markets, media, and period stated in the scope. They are not automatically buying every concept explored, the working files, or the right to have another studio extend the system, unless the agreement says so.

State the use in plain language in the scope: products and SKUs covered, territories, channels (retail, ecommerce, advertising), and duration if the work is campaign based. Packaging is usually bought outright for the product it was designed for, and that is fine; what must not happen is silent expansion, where packaging artwork becomes the whole brand identity, the advertising system, and the social template library without anyone pricing the expansion.

### Source files
Native source files (layered design files, working illustrations, editable templates) are a separate asset with a separate price, for three practical reasons: they took the whole project to build, they transfer the ability to extend the work without the studio, and they carry third party licence complications (fonts, stock, plugins) that the client must solve on their side.

Default position: final production files are included; native source files are excluded and available at a stated price. Price bases that work: a percentage of the design fee, a fixed fee per deliverable family, or inclusion only in the Best tier. Whatever the basis, write it before the project starts. A source file request at handover, with no prior agreement, is a negotiation the studio will have tired and under time pressure. See the source file decision tree.

### Buyouts
A buyout means broader rights: all concepts, full assignment, no restrictions. It is legitimate and it is expensive, because the studio is selling future option value. Price it as its own line, never fold it silently into the design fee. If the client only needs one specific extension (for example the right to adapt the system to a sister brand), sell that extension, not the universe.

### Unused concepts
Concepts the client did not choose remain the studio's. This is standard, valuable (they seed future work), and a frequent surprise to clients, so say it in the scope, warmly and early: "You are buying the route you choose. The routes you did not choose stay with us, and we may reuse the thinking elsewhere, never for a direct competitor while we work together" (adapt the competitor promise to the studio's real policy, or omit it).

## Part 5. Rush fees

Rush is a price signal, not an apology. Work that displaces other clients, compresses review cycles, or requires out of hours work costs more because it costs more: rescheduling, overtime, error risk.

Mechanics: define rush in the rate sheet (for example a timeline shorter than the studio's standard for that scope, or a fixed launch date that forces weekend work), apply the multiplier to the affected phases (not automatically the whole project, unless the whole project is compressed), and quote the rush fee as a visible line. A visible rush line frequently produces a calmer outcome than any discount negotiation: the client discovers the deadline was softer than claimed and chooses the standard timeline at the standard price. That is the fee working as intended.

Never hide rush inside a padded base fee. If the deadline softens later, a hidden pad cannot be gracefully removed; a line item can.

## Part 6. Kill fees

Projects die: budgets freeze, champions leave, ranges get cut. A kill fee structure agreed at signing turns a painful event into an administrative one.

Structure by stage, because value delivered rises by stage:
- Before concepts: lowest percentage of the remaining fee, plus costs incurred
- After concept approval: higher, because the core creative value has been delivered and approved
- After artwork or production begins: highest, because most of the cost is committed
Work completed is payable in full in all cases. On kill, the client receives the work they have paid for at the stage reached, with the usage terms applying to that work only; they do not receive rights to unfinished or unapproved routes. Drafting aid, counsel review.

Say the quiet part: the kill fee is not a penalty. It is how the studio reserves capacity and turns down other work. Clients who intend to proceed rarely object; clients who object strongly are telling you something about the project's real certainty, and that information is worth having before you start, not after.

## Part 7. Change order mechanics

A change is any work not in the deliverables inventory: extra SKUs, new formats, a different architecture direction after approval, additional naming rounds, updated regulated content that forces re-artwork, a new market's requirements.

Mechanics that work:
1. Name it early and neutrally. "That is an addition to the scope, here is what it costs and what it does to the date" is a complete, professional sentence. It is not an accusation.
2. Quote it in writing using the change order template, at the change order rate from the rate sheet.
3. Get written approval before starting. Email approval is fine if the studio's terms say so.
4. State the schedule effect in the same document. Price and date together, always, because approving the cost while assuming the date is how the next conflict is scheduled.
5. If declined, record it and continue the original scope warmly. A declined change order is the system working, and the record protects both sides' memory of what was asked.

The failure mode to avoid is work now, paperwork later. It feels client friendly in the moment and converts directly into unpaid work and a resentful studio, or an invoice surprise and a resentful client. Both outcomes cost more than the awkward thirty seconds of sending the change order.

## Part 8. Payment schedules and late terms

Tie payments to milestones the client controls the timing of (approvals), not only to calendar dates the studio controls. Standard pattern:
- Deposit on signing, before work starts. No deposit, no start; this single rule prevents most bad debt in small studios.
- Milestone payment at concept approval.
- Milestone payment at artwork approval.
- Balance before release of final production files. File release is the studio's one clean leverage point; use it calmly and state it in the scope so it is never a surprise.
Invoice terms from the rate sheet (for example 14 or 30 days), with a late fee or interest clause and a pause right: work pauses when an invoice is overdue beyond a stated number of days, and dates move accordingly. Drafting aid, counsel review.

For large organisations with fixed 60 or 90 day payment runs, do not pretend the terms are 30. Price the reality: either accept their terms as a cost of that client tier (and start work only on a purchase order), or structure milestones so the studio's exposure at any moment is small. What you must not do is sign 30 day terms with a client whose accounts payable department pays at 90 and discover it at invoice two. Ask in discovery: "What is your actual payment run?" Their answer is a scoping input.

## Part 9. Scope creep defence language

Scope creep is rarely malice. It is enthusiasm, internal politics, and the genuine difficulty of seeing a project's edges from inside it. The defence is language used early, kindly, and consistently:

- At kickoff: "The scope lists 12 SKUs. If the range grows, we will price the additions as a small change order so you can decide with the numbers in front of you."
- At the first small ask: "Happy to. That sits outside the current scope, so I will send a one page change order today; it is [amount] and it moves delivery by [days]. Shout if you would rather swap it for something already in scope."
- The swap offer is the pressure valve. Many asks die when the client sees the trade, and the ones that survive are real priorities the studio gets paid for.
- At feedback: "To keep us inside the two included rounds, could you consolidate the team's comments into one set from [named decision maker]? Contradictory comments across separate rounds is where timelines go."
- In writing, always. Verbal agreements about scope are where memories diverge. A one line email ("Confirming we will add X for Y, delivery moves to Z") is enough.

Tone rule: never use the phrase "out of scope" as a rebuke. Use it as a category with a price and a choice attached.

## Part 10. Margin guardrails

- Build every fixed fee bottom up from estimated days at rate sheet rates, then sanity check it against the deliverables count. If the two disagree, find out why before sending.
- Project management and client handling time are real. Load them explicitly in the estimate (common patterns: a percentage of design time, or a per phase allowance), even if they are not shown as a client line.
- Keep an internal contingency for estimation error on unfamiliar work. Contingency is not shown to the client and is not a slush fund for free changes; change orders are still quoted.
- Track estimate versus actual on every project, briefly, at close. The rate sheet stays theoretical; the estimate history is what makes the next price accurate.
- Watch the three classic leaks: uncapped revision rounds, unpriced production liaison, and source files given away at handover. A studio that fixes only those three usually recovers more margin than any rate rise would give it.

## Part 11. Discount rules

One rule sits above all others: trade scope, never rate. A rate discount teaches the client that the rate was negotiable fiction, and every future project starts from the discounted number. A scope trade teaches them the menu is real: less money buys fewer things, and the full menu is still there next time.

Supporting rules:
- If the budget is genuinely fixed, design to it openly: "For that figure, here is the scope that works" and present it as the Good tier, with the fuller tiers visible. Clients respect a studio that designs to a budget more than one that discounts under pressure.
- Never discount in the same conversation the ask arrives in. Take it away, rework the scope, return with a structure. Speed reads as softness.
- Concessions, when truly needed, are traded, not given: faster payment, a longer commitment, a case study permission, a referral introduction. Get something that is not money for the money.
- Volume belongs in structure (retainer, multi phase commitment), not in per project haggling.
- Procurement benchmarking against day rates is answered with the deliverables list and the outcomes, plus the scope trade offer. See edge cases.
- The founder sets the floor. The person sending the proposal should know exactly which scope trades they may make and which need approval, and that boundary lives in the rate sheet.

## Closing note

None of this is about being difficult. Clients prefer studios whose scope they can understand, whose invoices never surprise them, and whose yes means the work will actually arrive. Clear scope is a sales asset. Price it, write it, and let it do the selling.
