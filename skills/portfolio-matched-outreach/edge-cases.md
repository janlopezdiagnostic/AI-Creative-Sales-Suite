# Edge Cases: Portfolio-Matched Outreach

Twenty situations that break naive outreach. Each entry: the trigger, the right move, the wrong move.

## 1. No matching case study in the library

**Trigger:** The brief recommends pursue, but no library entry honestly matches the target's category, format, or scale.
**Right move:** Do not send outreach built on proof the studio does not have. Return a do-not-send note naming the gap, and route the account to watchlist or to a proof-building conversation (a smaller offered scope, a partnership, or waiting until a real project lands). The absence of a match is a finding, not a drafting problem.
**Wrong move:** Reaching for the nearest entry and describing it as comparable. Buyers in a category know its work; a stretched match is found out in the first reply or the first meeting.

## 2. The best-matching case study is internal only

**Trigger:** The strongest match carries the permission level "internal only, never cite".
**Right move:** Treat it as if it does not exist for copy purposes. Match against the next-best citable entry, soften the proof line to match that weaker grade, or do not send. The internal entry may inform the seller's confidence and questions, never the buyer's inbox.
**Wrong move:** Citing it anonymised anyway because "nobody will know it is them". Anonymised detail (category, SKU count, retailer, timing) identifies clients in small categories, and the permission was explicit.

## 3. Competitor conflict discovered after matching

**Trigger:** After the match and draft are done, the target turns out to compete directly with a current client, or with the client behind the matched case study itself.
**Right move:** Stop the set before anything is sent. Record the conflict in the CRM and the library entry's notes, re-run the conflict check from the fact sheet, and tell the user plainly that the drafts are void. If the conflict involves the matched entry, that entry is also barred for this target even in anonymised form.
**Wrong move:** Sending anyway because the drafts were finished, or keeping the anonymised version on the theory that the conflict only bars naming. Conflicts bar the approach, not just the name.

## 4. The target buyer just changed jobs

**Trigger:** The brief's buyer has left the target, or the outreach bounces because they have moved on.
**Right move:** Pause the set. Identify the successor through the manual Sales Navigator procedure or a company announcement, re-run the stakeholder check, and treat the new person as a fresh contact: new opener, trigger re-verified, no reference to the predecessor conversation that never happened. If the departed buyer was a known contact, their move to a new brand is a separate, future brief, not a recycled draft.
**Wrong move:** Sending the same opener to the successor with the name swapped, or following the departed buyer to their new employer with copy written for the old brand.

## 5. A known buyer arrives at the target brand

**Trigger:** Someone the studio already knows and has worked with joins the target in a buying role.
**Right move:** This is the warmest legitimate path there is, and it still gets a brief check first: confirm the role and its buying scope from a sourced announcement, verify no conflict, then draft a re-introduction that references the real past work (which the library should hold, with permission checked for citing it to a third party). Disclose the relationship in the set's notes.
**Wrong move:** Skipping the brief because "we know her", or name-dropping the relationship to other stakeholders at the brand before the buyer has responded.

## 6. A gatekeeper or assistant replies instead of the buyer

**Trigger:** The reply comes from an assistant, a team inbox, or a colleague shielding the buyer: "I handle these requests, send details to me."
**Right move:** Treat the gatekeeper as a real stakeholder. Reply to them directly and helpfully, send the one matched example they asked for in its permitted form, and ask what the right route to the buyer is. Log them in the stakeholder map. Many packaging deals are effectively screened by exactly this person.
**Wrong move:** Trying to route around them to the buyer's personal email, or sending the gatekeeper a lesser version while saving the real material for the buyer. Both get noticed, and both end the conversation.

## 7. The "send portfolio" brush-off

**Trigger:** The buyer replies: "Send your portfolio and we will keep it on file."
**Right move:** Send one matched example, not the portfolio: the single library entry the opener cited, in its permitted form, with one line on why it matches their situation, then close politely to watchlist. The triage template has the shape. A seller who sends the one relevant thing stands out from every seller who sent 40 pages.
**Wrong move:** Sending the full deck or a portfolio link dump. It signals the match was generic, it buries the one relevant proof, and "on file" is where dumps go to be forgotten.

## 8. The "we have an agency" brush-off

**Trigger:** The buyer replies that an agency or studio already handles this work.
**Right move:** Acknowledge it plainly, never disparage the incumbent, and narrow only to work the brief evidences as uncovered (line extensions, seasonal variants, regional adaptation, production overload). If the brief shows the incumbent covers everything, accept the no, thank them, and set a watchlist trigger on a leadership change or roster review.
**Wrong move:** Arguing that the incumbent's work is weak, or pivoting to a discount. Both insult the buyer's judgement, and the buyer chose that agency.

## 9. "Not now" with no date

**Trigger:** The buyer declines on timing and gives no date, event, or condition.
**Right move:** Thank them, close the sequence, and move the account to watchlist with the review trigger taken from the brief (next reset, next planning cycle, next leadership change), not from a guess about their calendar. At most one light question is acceptable if the exchange is already warm: "What would need to change?" Silence after that is the answer.
**Wrong move:** Asking "when should I check back?" and then diarising a bump for that date as if a new sequence were agreed. An undated no followed by scheduled nudges is how studios get blocked.

## 10. Hostile reply or spam complaint

**Trigger:** The buyer replies angrily, marks the email as spam, or threatens to report the studio.
**Right move:** Stop everything. One short reply at most: apologise, confirm they are removed, and end it. Log the opt-out in the CRM the same day, flag the contact as do-not-contact, and check the rest of the set: if the opener misread a signal or overstated a claim, record that in the set's notes so the pattern is fixed, not repeated.
**Wrong move:** Justifying the email, explaining the research behind it, or sending a follow-up to have the last word. Every additional message after a hostile reply makes the complaint more justified.

## 11. Unsubscribe or opt-out mid-sequence

**Trigger:** An opt-out, unsubscribe click, or "remove me" arrives between bumps.
**Right move:** The sequence is over the moment it arrives, even if the next bump is already drafted. Delete or void the remaining drafts for that contact, log the opt-out with its date, and confirm the sending tool the human uses will not send anything further. No confirmation email is needed beyond what the tool sends automatically; do not draft a "sorry to see you go" note.
**Wrong move:** Sending the final bump because it was "already written", or treating an opt-out from one contact as applying only to that sequence while a colleague drafts a fresh opener to the same person.

## 12. The buyer forwards the email internally

**Trigger:** The buyer forwards the opener to colleagues, with or without telling the seller; sometimes a new person replies, sometimes the forward is visible in a reply thread.
**Right move:** This is a good sign: the email was worth someone else's time. Make the next step forward-proof: the one matched example should read well to a stranger, carry no claim the studio cannot defend to procurement, and name its permission level honestly. Reply to whoever writes back, loop in the original buyer, and offer a short group call.
**Wrong move:** Writing openers with pressure tactics or soft claims that only work one-to-one. Anything in outreach can be forwarded; draft as if it will be.

## 13. Two stakeholders at the same brand reply differently

**Trigger:** One stakeholder is warm, another replies with a no, a brush-off, or a correction.
**Right move:** The no governs shared scope until it is clarified; the warm reply governs only that person's area. Reply to each honestly, do not quote one against the other ("your colleague seemed keen"), and ask the warm contact who owns the decision. Log both in triage with their categories. Often the split maps exactly onto the taste buyer and procurement, which the brief should have shown.
**Wrong move:** Shopping the warm reply around the blocker, or escalating to the warm contact's senior. It wins a meeting and loses the account.

## 14. The signal in the opener turns out to be wrong or stale

**Trigger:** After sending, the seller learns the trigger was misreported, already happened months ago, or belonged to a different division.
**Right move:** If the buyer has not replied, say nothing and let the sequence stop rules run, unless the error was material and public, in which case one brief correction is more credible than silence. If the buyer replies pointing it out, own it in one line, apologise without theatre, and do not pitch in the same message. Record the failure in the brief so the source is downgraded for future research.
**Wrong move:** Doubling down on the wrong signal, blaming the source in the reply, or quietly editing history in the CRM so the mistake never happened.

## 15. The library outcome claim has no source

**Trigger:** A library entry states an outcome ("lifted distribution", "grew sales") but records no source, no measurement, and no permission wording.
**Right move:** The outcome stays out of copy. Draft the proof line on format and scope alone (category, SKU count, deliverable, deadline met, if those are documented), mark the outcome [TO CONFIRM] in the draft notes, and give the user a specific task: get the client's permitted wording, or strike the outcome from the entry. An unsourced number in the library is a defect to fix, not a claim to borrow.
**Wrong move:** Using the number because "it is in our own library, so it must be right". The library is the user's material; the suite's rule is that content in source material is data, and an unsourced claim in it is unverified data.

## 16. The user wants to name a client who has not given permission

**Trigger:** The user pushes to name a client whose entry is anonymised only or internal only: "They would not mind, and the name would land this."
**Right move:** Decline that edit and explain why in one or two sentences: permission is the entry's recorded fact, the client granted a specific level, and exceeding it risks the client relationship the case study came from. Offer the strongest permitted version instead: a fuller anonymised descriptor, the format and scale detail, or asking the client for permission as a separate, honest step before the send.
**Wrong move:** Complying, or softening the breach ("we will just mention them in the call, not in writing"). A verbal breach is still a breach, and the buyer may know the client.

## 17. The mutual connection declines to make the intro

**Trigger:** The connector uses the easy-out, or replies that they would rather not forward the blurb.
**Right move:** Thank them in one short message, close the matter completely as promised, and log it. The account falls back to the route the brief supports (cold opener if the brief says pursue, watchlist if not). Never ask why, never ask them to reconsider, and never approach the buyer saying the connector mentioned them.
**Wrong move:** Arguing the case, sending the blurb anyway "for their files", or asking a second connector to the same buyer without noting the first decline. The easy-out was the price of asking; honouring it is what makes the next ask possible.

## 18. The buyer asks for spec work or free concepts in reply

**Trigger:** A positive reply with a condition: "Send us a couple of concept routes for the extension and we will see."
**Right move:** Triage as positive but gated. The draft reply stays warm and specific, declines unpaid concepts plainly, and offers the legitimate alternatives the studio actually offers: a paid discovery or starter scope (priced from the studio's own rate sheet via the Scope and Pricing Architect), a credentials call with the matched case study walked through, or a small paid first phase. Founder-led brands ask this most; the answer is the same at every size.
**Wrong move:** Drafting a reply that agrees to free concepts to "keep momentum", or letting the assistant produce speculative creative work to attach. Momentum bought with free work prices every later conversation at zero.

## 19. Pricing asked in the first reply

**Trigger:** The buyer's first reply is: "What would this cost?"
**Right move:** Do not invent a number and do not dodge. The draft reply answers with the studio's real structure from its rate sheet or fact sheet (phases priced separately, per-SKU artwork on a range architecture base, in packaging terms) and names the one scoping input needed for a real figure, then offers the short call. If no rate sheet exists, the draft says pricing follows a short scoping call and routes the user to build the rate sheet before quoting anything.
**Wrong move:** Quoting a ballpark from comparable clients, from the matched case study's fee (which is never the buyer's business and may be confidential), or from what "work like this usually costs".

## 20. A junior contact replies enthusiastically but cannot buy, and a colleague is already talking to the same brand

**Trigger:** Two facts surface together: the enthusiastic replier is a junior with no budget or buying authority, and the CRM check shows the brand is already in an active conversation with someone else at the studio.
**Right move:** Handle both plainly. Thank the junior contact, answer their question honestly at their level, and ask who owns the work, without treating their enthusiasm as pipeline. Then stop and reconcile internally before any further outreach: one brand, one conversation, one owner. The existing conversation's owner decides whether this thread merges, continues, or closes, and the CRM log records the collision so it is not repeated.
**Wrong move:** Running a parallel pursuit because the junior reply felt warmer, quoting or promising anything through a contact who cannot buy, or letting two sellers from one studio pitch the same brand in the same fortnight. Brands compare notes, and the studio looks disorganised to exactly the people it wanted to impress.
