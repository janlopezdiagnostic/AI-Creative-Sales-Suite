# Playbook: Portfolio-Matched Outreach for Creative Services

This playbook is the method behind the Portfolio-Matched Outreach skill: matching, opener anatomy, follow-ups, channel variants, personalisation, anti-spam and anti-slop rules, and volume trade-offs. It assumes a finished Skill 1 brief and a completed case-study library. Without those, there is no method to run.

A note on examples: every brand, studio, and person named in an example in this playbook is fictional and labelled fictional. Examples show structure only. They are never proof for a real account.

## The matching method

Outreach fails when proof does not match the problem. Buyers need work like their situation, bought by someone like them, for a reason like theirs. Matching is a scoring exercise, not a taste exercise. Run it the same way every time, record the scores, and let the grade control what you may draft.

Work from the brief first: category, format and deliverables in the hypothesis, buyer type, and the outcome the buyer values most. Score every plausible entry against that extract, not just the favourite.

### Relevance axes

Score each axis 0 to 3. Use the library entry and the brief, not enthusiasm and not memory.

**Category proximity.** 3: same category and same or adjacent subcategory. 2: adjacent category with the same buying logic and shelf context. 1: same broad sector but different shelf and constraints. 0: unrelated category. Buyers discount proof from worlds they do not recognise.

**Format and deliverable match.** 3: same format and deliverables the hypothesis names, at comparable scale, for example an 8 SKU range redesign matching an 8 to 12 SKU range redesign. 2: same deliverable family with a scale or format difference that does not change the core job, for example a range redesign matching a line extension into the same system. 1: adjacent deliverable only, for example identity work offered as proof for a packaging system job. 0: different deliverable with no credible bridge. Never let a beautiful identity project carry a structural packaging claim it did not do.

**Buyer-type match.** 3: bought by the same buyer type as the target, for example a brand director bought it and a brand director is the target. 2: a closely related type, for example a founder bought it and a founder-led target is now hiring its first brand director. 1: a different buying centre with different cares, for example a procurement-led panel purchase offered to a founder who buys on taste and speed. 0: a buyer type that undermines credibility with this target, or buyer type is not recorded in the library at all. If the library does not record buyer type, score 0 until the entry is completed. Unknown is not a neutral score.

**Outcome relevance.** 3: the library outcome, as recorded with its source, is the outcome this buyer values most, stated only as far as the source supports. 2: relevant but secondary for this buyer, or client-reported without permission to cite numbers, so the draft can state the kind of outcome but not a figure. 1: generic only (the client was pleased, the work launched) with no source supporting a specific claim. 0: no outcome recorded, outcome is internal only, or outcome has nothing to do with what this buyer needs. A score of 0 or 1 here caps what the proof line can say.

**Recency.** 3: completed within the last two years, or older but still in market in its designed form. 2: two to four years ago and still representative. 1: older than four years, or the category has moved on since it shipped. 0: superseded, withdrawn, or no longer offered in that form.

**Proof asset quality.** 3: assets on file and ready to show in context, shareable at the permission level. 2: assets exist but are limited, for example pack shots only, or pages needing small preparation. 1: claimed but not located, low quality, or out of context. 0: none available, or cannot be shared at the permission level. Without assets you cannot build a mini-portfolio reply from the entry.

### Match grades

Add the six scores. Maximum is 18. The total matters, and the minimums matter more.

**Strong match.** Total 14 to 18, with no axis at 0, category proximity at 2 or above, format and deliverable match at 2 or above, and outcome relevance at 2 or above. A strong match earns the full matched opener: trigger, relevance, one proof line with the strongest claim the library supports at its permission level, and a specific ask referencing the matched work directly.

**Partial match.** Total 9 to 13, or total 14 or above but one Strong minimum is missed, with no axis at 0 except possibly recency or asset quality at 1 handled with care. A partial match earns an observation-led opener only: trigger and a genuinely useful observation lead, proof softened to capability framing (kind of work, category context, no stretched claim), and no figure, result, or client name beyond what the library plainly supports. Record why the match is partial so the reviewer sees the gap before approving.

If category proximity is 0 or format match is 0, the entry cannot be graded above Partial regardless of total, and in most cases it should be graded None for this account. A high total built on buyer type and recency does not fix proof about different work in a different category.

**No match (None).** Total 8 or below, or every plausible entry fails a minimum above, or the only entries available are internal only, never cite. None is a finding, not a failure of effort. Record it plainly, with the scores, and stop.

Illustration, all fictional: Target Willow Lane Pantry (fictional), 6 SKU extension for a retailer reset. Harbour Pantry Sauces Extension (fictional) scores 3, 3, 2, 2, 3, 2, total 15, Strong. Northline Skincare Rebrand (fictional) totals 12 but category and format are 1, so it is not the primary match. Scores force the honest pick.

### The None rule

None means do not send generic outreach. There are exactly three options, and the human chooses between them with your recommendation recorded.

First, capability plus observation only, and only if a real relationship exists on record. Then a short note may name a specific observation and state capability in one line, with no case study claim. Without a real relationship, this does not apply.

Second, wait for a better trigger. Return the account to the watchlist with the trigger that would change the match or timing, and set the review date to that trigger.

Third, walk away. If the gap is structural, record do not pursue for outreach and file the scores.

Never borrow another client's results to fill a None. Never combine two weak entries into one implied strong result. Never stretch a weak match into a claim by changing the wording: calling an identity project a packaging system, calling internal feedback a client outcome, or naming an anonymised only client to add weight. If the library has a gap the studio keeps hitting, say so in the handoff. A library gap is useful commercial information. A stretched claim is a liability the buyer will find.

## Opener anatomy for taste-led outreach

Taste-led buyers decide in seconds whether a sender understands their world. The opener does four jobs in order, with no preamble, then stops. The structure is for email first. Channel variants adapt it, they do not replace its logic.

**Subject line (email only).** Short, specific, and honest about what is inside. Name the trigger or the work, not a trick. Good patterns, using fictional examples: "Willow Lane extension and the reset date" (fictional target), or "Pack system for the new dips range" (fictional work reference). Rules: no fake Re: or Fwd:, no question the email does not answer, no curiosity gap that collapses on opening, no buyer name stuffing, no all capitals, no promised result. If the subject line would embarrass the sender read aloud to the buyer, rewrite it.

**Line 1: the observed trigger.** One sentence. The dated signal from the brief, stated as observation, not as surveillance. It names what changed and, where it helps, when. Example shape, fictional: "I saw [Brand (fictional)] is taking its dips range into [Retailer (fictional)] this autumn, with reset submissions due ahead of that." Every element must trace to a brief signal with a source and a date. If the trigger cannot be stated in one clean sentence, the brief is too weak to draft from. Send it back.

**Line 2: why it matters, or the cost of getting it wrong.** One sentence, sometimes two short ones. This is the bridge between their change and creative work. Name a real consequence a practitioner would recognise: a pack designed for a screen having three seconds on a shelf, an extension straining an ageing hierarchy, a material change altering colour and standout, a deadline that does not move. Do not manufacture a problem the brief does not support. Do not use fear language. The test is whether a brand director would nod, not flinch.

**Line 3: the matched proof.** One case study, one line, only claims the library supports, at its permission level. Pattern: what the studio did, for what kind of client (named only if name freely), in what situation, with what supported outcome or asset offer. Example shape, fictional and anonymised only: "We rebuilt the range architecture and artwork for a premium sauces brand (fictional descriptor) taking 8 SKUs into national retail for the first time, and I can share the shelf photographs if useful." If the outcome is client-reported without permission to cite a figure, state the kind of outcome and offer the asset, do not state the figure. If the entry is internal only, never cite, this line cannot use it at all. In a partial match, this line becomes capability framing instead: kind of work and category context, with no implied result.

**Line 4: a low friction, specific ask.** One ask, small, named, and easy to refuse. Good asks: 20 minutes to walk through one teardown the studio has prepared, permission to send the matched shelf photographs, or a short call on a named day window to test whether the timing in the brief is right. Bad asks: "a quick call?" with no length, subject, or reason; "15 minutes to pick your brain"; a calendar link dumped with no context; a request for a full brief or a wider team meeting as the first step. Specificity is respect. "20 minutes" is a commitment the buyer can judge. "A quick call?" is an open-ended tax.

**Sign-off and sender credibility line.** Sign as the real sender, by name and role, from the sender identity input. One credibility line at most, and only if true and relevant: the sender's role on the matched project, or the studio's relevant specialism in one phrase from the fact sheet. Do not add awards lists, client names as a list, or a biography. The proof line already did the credibility work.

**Length discipline.** Opener email: under 100 words in the body, not counting subject line and sign-off. LinkedIn DM: under 60 words. If a draft runs long, the usual causes are two triggers instead of one, two case studies instead of one, or explanation that belongs in a meeting. Cut to one of each. Short is not a style preference. Short is what makes the match visible.

### What a taste-led opener is NOT

It is not a flattery opener. Do not open with praise for the brand, the pack, the founder, or a recent post, unless the brief records a specific, relevant observation that does real work in line 1. Empty flattery signals a template.

It is not "I hope this email finds you well" or any other filler greeting that delays the trigger. Go straight to the observation. A plain "Hello [Name]" is enough.

It is not fake personalisation from scraped trivia. A school, a hobby, a holiday post, a football team, or a family detail scraped from a profile is not personalisation. It is intrusion, and it fails the defensibility test below.

It is not name-dropping. Do not name a client without library permission, imply an endorsement that was never given, or list clients as social proof in a first message. One matched proof, at its permission level, outweighs a list.

It is not a capabilities brochure in miniature. Services lists, process descriptions, and studio history belong nowhere in an opener.

Worked opener, all details fictional, for structure only: Subject: "Extension packs and the autumn reset" (fictional). Body: "Hello Ana (fictional), I saw Willow Lane Pantry (fictional) is adding a dips range ahead of an autumn reset. Extensions into a new format are where range hierarchy usually strains first, especially at shelf distance. We rebuilt architecture and artwork for a premium sauces brand (fictional descriptor) in the same position, 8 SKUs into national retail, and the shelf photographs show the system clearly. Would 20 minutes next week to look at one teardown of your current range be useful? Tom Ellis (fictional), Founder, Studio North (fictional)." It sits under the limit because it carries one trigger, one consequence, one proof, and one ask, and nothing else.

## Follow-up sequences

Most replies come from follow-ups. Most bad reputation does too. Each bump must add new value the buyer can use even if they never reply. Draft the standard three bumps with the opener, as one set for review. No more as standard, and no bump improvised later.

**Cadence guidance.** Space bumps in business days. Default: Bump 1 at 4 to 5 business days after the opener, Bump 2 at 5 to 7 business days after Bump 1, Bump 3 at 7 to 10 business days after Bump 2. Adjust for the brief window: a hard deadline may justify the shorter end, a distant seasonal window the longer end. Never send two bumps in the same week unless a dated deadline makes timing the substance. The human sets send times. Stop rules in `decision-trees.md` Tree 3 override this cadence.

**Standing alone.** Each bump must stand alone if earlier messages were never read. Restate in one short clause who the sender is, what triggered contact, and what the ask is. A buyer who opens only Bump 2 must understand it in one read.

**The banned move.** No bump may say "just bumping this", "circling back", "floating this to the top of your inbox", "checking in", or any equivalent whose only content is a reminder that an earlier message exists. If a planned bump has no new value to add, do not draft it. Skip to the break-up form of Bump 3 or stop the sequence and record why.

### Bump 1: the teardown insight

Bump 1 adds a teardown insight: a specific, genuinely useful observation about the target's current pack, site, or shelf presence, drawn from material the brief or the reviewer can legitimately point to. One observation, stated precisely, with its implication in one line. For packaging, observations that qualify include hierarchy at distance versus at thumbnail size, variant differentiation within a range, legibility of a mandatory element after a material change, or blocking against the immediate competitive set on shelf.

Rules: it must be useful if the buyer never speaks to the studio, so they could take it to their incumbent or internal team. It must not be a criticism ambush: no list of flaws, no scored takedown, no implication the current work is failing. State it as a practitioner would to a peer, name what you noticed and why it matters for the trigger, and connect it to the matched proof only if the connection is natural in one clause. Offer the fuller teardown (one teardown, not a full audit) as the ask. If no defensible observation can be made from legitimate material, say so and replace Bump 1 with the strongest news item planned for Bump 2, rather than inventing an insight.

### Bump 2: relevant news

Bump 2 adds relevant news: a development since the opener that changes or sharpens the original trigger. Sources follow the Skill 1 hierarchy: a signal development (launch date confirmed, range size changed), a category move (a competitor refresh, a retailer announcement for the category), or a retailer or regulatory date that now bears on the target's formats or market. The news must be dated and sourced in working notes, exactly as a brief signal would be. The draft states the news in one or two lines, why it matters for this buyer in one line, and repeats a small, specific ask.

Rules: do not present old news as new. Do not use general industry content (a report, a trend piece, a studio blog post) as a pretext unless it names a date or a rule that directly affects this target. Do not attach documents the buyer did not ask for. A link to a primary source (the retailer's announcement, the regulation) is acceptable where the channel allows it and the human approves it. A link to the studio's own marketing is not news.

### Bump 3: the seasonal or calendar hook, or the break-up note

Bump 3 has two legitimate forms. Choose one at drafting time, based on the brief, and draft only that form as standard.

Form A, the seasonal or calendar hook, applies where a real calendar fact creates a natural reason to return: the next retailer reset cycle for the category, a seasonal range deadline worked backwards from shelf date, a regulatory compliance date, or the target's own planning cycle where the brief records it. State the date, state the working backwards logic in one line (artwork lock minus production minus design equals a start date), and make the ask a timing check, not a pitch. Invented urgency fails here. If the date does not affect this buyer, it is not a hook.

Form B, the clean break-up note, applies where no calendar hook exists or the window in the brief has passed without engagement. It closes the sequence in three or four lines: assume the timing or fit is not right, name the trigger that would make a future conversation sensible, give the buyer a clean way to reopen contact, and stop. No guilt, no "this is my last email" theatre, no false scarcity, no new claim at the exit. A good break-up note is the message most likely to be forwarded or remembered when the trigger does fire, which is why it must leave the door genuinely open and the record clean.

After Bump 3, the sequence ends. Log the outcome, hand the account to Revive and Referral with a future date per `decision-trees.md`, and do not draft a Bump 4.

## Channel variants

The same match and proof must survive every channel. Only length, form, and inclusions change. Draft only the channel `decision-trees.md` selects. Cross-channel repetition in the same week reads as automation.

### Email (default, fullest form)

Email is the default because it carries the full anatomy, allows a subject line, and creates a clean thread for follow-ups and replies. Rules: opener body under 100 words, bumps also short (as a guide under 90 words each, because a long bump signals a new pitch rather than new value). Plain text spirit: no heavy formatting, no banners, no embedded images in the opener or bumps. Assets are sent only when the buyer asks or grants permission, or as the matched mini-portfolio in reply handling, prepared and approved by a human. One ask per message. The sender is the named human from their real address. The skill drafts, the human sends from their own account, always.

### LinkedIn DM and InMail (shorter, no attachments)

LinkedIn is appropriate where the buyer is publicly active there, where no legitimate email route exists, or where prior contact happened there. It is manual only: the user pastes or exports profile or thread material, you analyse and draft, the human sends by hand. Never suggest scraping, automation, or bulk InMail campaigns.

Rules: opener under 60 words. No attachments, ever, on this channel. No links in the first message unless the buyer has asked for one. Compress the anatomy, do not drop its logic: trigger in one clause, relevance in one clause, proof in one line at its permission level, ask in one line. The ask should usually be permission to send the matched example by email, or a 20 minute call, stated just as specifically as on email. Follow-ups follow the same three bump value rules and cadence logic, shortened further: if the value does not fit in a short message, it belongs on email with permission, not in a long DM.

**Connection request note variant.** Where no connection exists, the note is a separate, shorter form: under 300 characters including spaces. It must state who the sender is, the trigger in brief, and a reason to connect that is honest about intent. It must not carry the full pitch, a proof claim with a figure, or a meeting ask. Example shape, fictional, for character discipline only: "Hello Ana (fictional), I lead Studio North (fictional). I saw Willow Lane (fictional) is extending into dips ahead of the autumn reset. We have done similar range work. Would value connecting." If the request is declined or ignored, that is an outcome. Log it and follow the stop rules. Do not route around it with a second account or a colleague's account.

### Warm introduction request (to the mutual connection, not the target)

Where the tree selects this route, the draft goes to the mutual connection, not the target. Its job is to make helping easy, safe, and refusable.

Rules: address the connection by name, state the ask in the first two lines (an introduction to the named buyer at the named account, for a named reason tied to the trigger), and give them the forwardable paragraph below. State plainly that a refusal or delay costs nothing. Do not ask the connection to endorse the studio's results or make claims from the library on the studio's behalf. Do not draft a message to the target and ask the connection to send it as if it were theirs.

**Make it forwardable.** Include one paragraph the connection can paste unchanged into their own message to the buyer. That paragraph should contain: who the sender is, the trigger, one matched proof line at its permission level, and the small specific ask. Keep it to four or five lines. The connection should need to add only a greeting and, if they wish, one personal line of their own.

**Give them an easy out.** Include a line that explicitly releases them: if the timing is wrong, if they do not know the buyer well enough, or if they would rather not, a simple no is complete and will not be followed up. Then honour it. If they decline, thank them, log it, and return to the channel tree for the next legitimate route. Never draft a guilt follow-up to a connection who declined.

If the introduction happens, reference it truthfully in line 1 of the opener, keep the rest of the anatomy, and log the connection as origin.

## Personalisation rules

Personalisation here has a narrow definition: material from the brief and the library that makes the message specific to this account, this trigger, and this buyer role. Everything else is decoration or intrusion.

Permitted personalisation: the dated trigger and its source context, the buyer's role and, where the brief names them with a source, their tenure or remit as it bears on the trigger, the target's current range, format, retailer, or material facts as recorded in the brief, and the matched library entry. That is the full list. If a line does not come from the brief or the library, it needs a specific justification recorded in working notes, or it comes out.

Prohibited personalisation: personal trivia scraped or browsed from profiles (education, hobbies, family, location history, personal posts), compliments about personal content, references to mutual interests outside work, and any detail the buyer has not put into a professional context relevant to this purchase. The fact that a detail is publicly visible does not make it fair or effective to use.

The test, applied to every line before handoff: could the sender defend every line if the buyer asked how they knew it? A line passes if the answer is a professional source the sender can name without embarrassment: the brief's dated signal, the buyer's public professional announcement, the pack on shelf, the retailer's published reset information. A line fails if the answer involves browsing a personal profile, inferring a private fact, or a data tool the sender would not describe to the buyer. Failed lines come out, however clever they read.

Use the buyer's name exactly as the source spells it. Do not shorten, Anglicise, or add a title the source does not use. If uncertain, use the role rather than a guessed name.

## Anti-spam and anti-AI-slop rules

These rules protect the studio's domain and reputation. They are hard rules in practice.

**No volume blasting.** Do not draft near-identical openers for many accounts with the name and trigger swapped. Each draft set starts from its own brief and its own match scores. If two sets could swap buyers without anyone noticing, at least one of them has failed.

**No purchased lists worked cold without research briefs.** A purchased or rented list is not an input to this skill. An account enters outreach only through a Skill 1 brief with a pursue recommendation. If a list is supplied, route selected accounts through Skill 1 first, do not draft for the list.

**No fake Re: subject lines, no fake threads, no fabricated familiarity.** Do not write "great meeting you at" an event the sender did not attend with the buyer, "following up on our conversation" where none happened, or "as [person] suggested" where they did not. Prior contact may only be referenced where the sender identity input records it as real.

**No slop phrasing.** Language model tells and generic sales filler signal automation at a glance. Avoid, at minimum: "I hope this email finds you well", "in today's fast-paced market", "leverage", "synergy", "game-changer", "take your brand to the next level", "revolutionise your packaging", "unlock", "supercharge", "delve", "navigate the landscape", "in the ever-evolving world of", "touch base", "circle back" without new value, "just checking in", "I would love to pick your brain", "quick question" as a subject for a message that asks for a meeting, exclamation marks in a first message, and any sentence that could appear unchanged in outreach for software, recruitment, or financial services. This list is illustrative, not exhaustive. The underlying rule is one human voice: write as the named sender would speak to a peer they respect, in short plain sentences, with concrete nouns (SKU, shelf, dieline, reset, substrate) over abstract ones (journey, story, ecosystem) unless the record supports the abstract word.

**One human voice, one real sender.** Every draft is in the voice of the named sender, consistent across opener, bumps, and reply drafts for that account. Do not switch register between messages. Do not add a second sign-off, a team signature block with five links, or a postscript manufactured to look personal. A postscript is allowed only where it carries real, additional relevant information the human approves.

**Respect opt-outs at once.** Any opt-out, unsubscribe request, or clear refusal ends the sequence immediately, on every channel, per the stop rules. Log it as do-not-contact where the buyer asks for no further contact. Do not draft a final message to argue, clarify, or ask why, beyond the single apology and removal confirmation the reply tree permits for hostile replies.

## Volume and quality trade-offs

The honest maths of taste-led outreach is uncomfortable for anyone measured on activity. This method produces fewer touches than sequence-at-scale outreach, by design. One account requires a brief, a scored match, an opener, three bumps, and reply handling, all reviewed by a senior human before sending.

What the trade buys: each touch carries a dated reason, matched proof, and a specific ask, so replying is low cost and being wrong is contained. It does not buy any promised reply rate, meeting rate, or pipeline figure. Judge it on replies that become scoped conversations, timing quality, and absence of complaints or stretched claims.

**When a studio should not do outreach at all this week.** Two conditions pause outreach entirely, even where briefs are ready. First, no match: if the week's accounts all grade None, the correct output is library and watchlist work, not forced sends. Sending anyway teaches buyers that the studio's targeting means nothing. Second, no capacity to deliver: if the studio cannot staff the work within the window the brief names, at the standard the matched case study implies, do not open the conversation. A meeting won into a capacity failure damages the category reputation the library depends on. Record the pause and its reason, keep the briefs' review dates live, and resume when the condition clears.



**Record-keeping.** Log every touch and every outcome. For each account, the record holds: the brief identifier and recommendation, the match scores and grade, the primary and backup entries used, the channel selected and its reasoning, the draft set as approved by the human, what was actually sent and when (recorded by the human sender), each reply with its triage category, meetings booked and their handoff to the Discovery Call Kit, opt-outs and do-not-contact additions, referrals and their origin, and the final outcome with its date, including handoff to Revive and Referral after a completed sequence with no reply. The skill proposes log entries as drafts for confirmation. The human confirms them. Complete records make the next brief faster and the library gap visible.
