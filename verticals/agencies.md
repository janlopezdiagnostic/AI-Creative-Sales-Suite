# Vertical Layer: Agencies

Load this file alongside any core skill when the seller is a multi-discipline creative agency selling integrated work (brand, campaign, digital, social, content under one roof). It overrides generic examples with agency reality. Where this file and a core skill disagree, this file wins for generalist agency work.

## The buyer map

- **CMO (the relationship buyer).** Owns the roster and the big appointments. Judges strategic thinking, chemistry, and whether the agency makes them look good internally. An AOR decision is a CMO's reputation spent in public; price and scope must protect them as much as serve them.
- **Brand teams (the day-to-day buyers).** Brand managers and marketing leads who brief campaigns and live with the output. They judge responsiveness and craft, and they churn faster than CMOs: today's brand manager is next year's contact at another company.
- **Procurement and marketing procurement panels.** Run rosters, rate cards, and panel reviews at larger advertisers. Buy by benchmarking, standard terms, and risk paperwork. Procurement rarely chooses the agency, but it can absolutely un-choose one on rate, terms, or compliance.
- **Intermediaries / search consultants.** The gatekeepers on formal pitches: they run longlists, shortlists, chemistry meetings, and the pitch itself. They are not the buyer, but they control access to the buyer, and their process rules are real constraints, not decoration.

## Deal shapes

- **Agency of record (AOR) retainer:** the flagship appointment. A defined scope of ongoing work for a monthly or quarterly fee, usually won through a pitch and reviewed annually. Price from a service menu and expected volume, never from "whatever last year cost plus a bit."
- **Campaign projects:** discrete, dated, often the trial that precedes an AOR conversation. Also the form most work takes with mid-size clients who will never appoint an AOR.
- **Roster / panel positions:** a place on an approved supplier roster for a category of work. Low commitment on both sides, volume not guaranteed. The panel terms (rate card, payment terms, IP clauses) are the real deal being signed; the work comes later, or doesn't.
- **Project-to-retainer and discipline expansion:** the growth path. A campaign client adds social, then content, then a retained team. Each discipline added is a new scope with its own rate basis, not an informal stretch of the original fee.
- **Pitch culture, priced honestly:** pitches come in three kinds. Paid pitches (a fee or honorarium, common for large formal processes), partially covered (expenses or a token fee), and unpaid speculative pitches, still the default for smaller briefs. The suite treats pitch spend as a budgeted business decision with a walk-away line, not a moral failing or a free good.

## Calendars and timing signals

- **Annual planning:** most large clients set marketing budgets and roster plans in a fixed annual cycle, commonly closing in the autumn for the following year `[VERIFY PER CLIENT]`. AOR ambitions raised after planning closes wait a year or arrive underfunded. Ask when planning closes before proposing retainer shapes.
- **Pitch cycles:** pitches cluster around contract expiries, CMO arrivals, and planning season. A CMO appointment is again the highest-receptivity window (the first 90 days, strongest roughly days 14 to 90, a practitioner rule of thumb), and roster reviews are often announced to incumbents before the market hears anything, so relationship coverage of existing clients is also a timing skill.
- **Roster and panel reviews:** formal reviews run on the client's cycle (often every 2-3 years for panels). Incumbency is an advantage only if the account has been visibly maintained; quiet accounts get reviewed out.
- **Intermediary calendars:** search consultants run processes to published timetables: RFI, chemistry, shortlist, pitch, appointment, with weeks between stages. Missing an RFI deadline by a day ends the pursuit regardless of fit.

## Vocabulary the skill must use correctly

Agency of record (AOR), roster, panel, rate card, retainer, scope of work (SOW), master services agreement (MSA), RFI / RFP, chemistry meeting, longlist / shortlist, search consultant / intermediary, incumbent, sell-in (internal), blended rate, utilisation, scope creep, workstream, retainer burn.

## Proof types that land

Case studies with business outcomes the client permits citing, category experience that maps to the brief, chemistry (the actual team in the room, not the pitch theatre team), intermediary references, evidence of retained relationships that lasted (longevity is the AOR proof). Never cite client results you cannot source; mark anything the agency claims but has not documented as `[TO CONFIRM]` in drafts.

## Commercial mechanics reality (where naive scopes bleed)

Integrated work crosses disciplines with different cost bases: strategy, creative, design, production, social, and media-adjacent work do not carry the same margin or the same revision behaviour. An integrated scope priced as one blended blob hides which workstream is losing money until the quarter closes. Build integrated scopes discipline by discipline, with a rate basis and revision rule per workstream, then present one number if the client wants one number. The MSA is where the money terms actually live (payment terms, IP ownership of working files vs final assets, kill terms, liability): negotiate it once, carefully, because every later SOW inherits it.

## Pricing norms (structure, not numbers)

Numbers always come from the agency's own rate card and rate sheet. Structures that are normal here: retainers priced from a service menu with a defined monthly capacity and an explicit overage rate, campaign fees by phase with production as a separate priced layer, blended rates used only where the mix is genuinely stable, panel rate cards with scheduled review dates (a rate card without a review date is a permanent discount), payment terms and IP/file terms set in the MSA and referenced by every SOW, pitch fees credited against the first project where the client agrees, and kill fees staged by phase in every SOW, not improvised at the bad news meeting.

## Agency-specific traps (feed these into the core skills' edge cases)

1. Unpaid pitch economics ignored: the agency pitches everything, wins its usual fraction, and the pitch cost is never counted against the account's first-year margin. Set a pitch budget, a qualification gate (budget exists, decision maker met, timeline real), and a walk-away line before the RFI lands.
2. Panel rate compression: procurement benchmarks the panel rate card against the cheapest supplier category and pushes all disciplines toward it at review. Defend with workstream-level rates and outcome proof, and never let a panel rate set for production work become the rate for strategy.
3. Search consultant dynamics misunderstood: the intermediary's process rules (page limits, team attendance, no side contact with the client) treated as optional. Breaking them quietly disqualifies agencies that thought they were being entrepreneurial.
4. Scope across disciplines priced as one blob: social overruns eat the strategy margin, nobody can see it, and the retainer review becomes an argument about feelings instead of numbers.
5. The pitch team is not the account team: clients discover it in month two, trust takes the hit, and the first roster review goes badly. Name the actual team in the pitch and price the work they will actually do.
6. Incumbent complacency: the AOR assumes the relationship is the moat while a challenger runs the research, outreach, and revive playbook in this suite against their client. Roster reviews are won in the quiet years, not at the review.
